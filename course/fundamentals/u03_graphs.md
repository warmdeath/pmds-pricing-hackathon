# F3 · Reading graphs and data | Reasoning

## F3.1 Axes, points and variables
A graph shows how one variable relates to another. The **horizontal axis (x)**
usually holds the variable that "drives" (time, price in data charts), the
**vertical axis (y)** the response. Economics has one famous exception: in
supply-and-demand diagrams **price is on the vertical axis** and quantity on
the horizontal axis, even though we think of price driving quantity.

Before reading a graph, check: what are the variables, what are the **units**
(€, %, thousands), what is the **period**, and where does each axis start.

> **Example.** A point (Q = 40, P = 12) on a demand curve reads: "at a price of
> €12, buyers want 40 units".

@mcq In a standard supply-and-demand diagram, the vertical axis shows…
+ price
- quantity | quantity is horizontal by convention.
- time
- profit
! Marshall's convention: price vertical.
@mcq A demand curve passes through (Q = 30, P = 8). This means…
+ at a price of 8, buyers want 30 units
- at a quantity of 8 the price is 30 | read the axes in the right order.
- 30 buyers pay 8 in total
- the firm earns 240 profit
! Read price on the vertical, quantity on the horizontal axis.
@mcq The first thing to check on any chart is…
+ what each axis measures, its units and where it starts
- the colours
- the title only
- the largest number
! Units and scale decide how to read the data.

## F3.2 Slope
The **slope** of a line = rise / run = Δy/Δx.

- Positive slope: y rises with x (supply).
- Negative slope: y falls as x rises (demand).
- Zero: horizontal; undefined: vertical.

For a straight line slope is constant; for a curve, slope changes and is
measured by the tangent at each point.

> **Example.** Between (Q = 10, P = 50) and (Q = 30, P = 40), slope =
> (40 − 50)/(30 − 10) = **−0.5**: each extra unit demanded goes with a price
> 0.5 lower.

Slope depends on units (cents vs euros) — that's why economists prefer
elasticity (F5.8) for comparisons.

@num Slope between (2, 10) and (6, 22)?
= 3
! (22 − 10)/(6 − 2) = 12/4 = 3.
@num Slope between (Q=20, P=80) and (Q=60, P=60)?
= -0.5
x 0.5 | price falls as quantity rises: negative slope.
! (60 − 80)/(60 − 20) = −20/40.
@mcq A horizontal line has slope…
+ 0
- 1
- infinite | that's a vertical line.
- −1
! No rise for any run.

## F3.3 Linear equations and intercepts
A straight line: **y = a + b·x**. The **intercept** a is the value of y when
x = 0; b is the slope.

Inverse demand P = 100 − 2Q: intercept 100 (the **choke price** where demand
falls to zero), slope −2. Rearranged as a demand function: Q = 50 − 0.5P
(quantity-intercept 50 = demand at a zero price).

> **Example.** Cost line C = 2,000 + 15Q: fixed cost 2,000 (intercept),
> marginal cost 15 per unit (slope). At Q = 100, C = 3,500.

@num Line y = 12 + 4x. Value of y at x = 7?
= 40
! 12 + 28 = 40.
@num Inverse demand P = 90 − 3Q. At what price does quantity demanded fall to zero (choke price)?
= 90
! Q = 0 ⇒ P = 90.
@num Same demand P = 90 − 3Q. Quantity demanded at a price of zero?
= 30
! 0 = 90 − 3Q ⇒ Q = 30.
@num Cost C = 5,000 + 12Q. Cost of 250 units?
= 8000
! 5,000 + 3,000.

## F3.4 Movements along vs shifts of a curve
A curve shows the relationship between the two variables **on its axes**,
holding everything else fixed.

- A change in a variable **on the axes** → **movement along** the curve.
- A change in **any other** variable → the whole curve **shifts**.

For demand drawn against price: a change in the good's own price moves you
along the curve; a change in income, tastes, other goods' prices or the number
of buyers shifts it.

> **Example.** The price of cinema tickets falls → more tickets bought
> (movement along). A hit film comes out → more tickets at every price (shift
> right).

@mcq The price of bread falls and people buy more bread. On the bread demand diagram this is…
+ a movement along the demand curve
- a shift right of demand | own price is on the axis.
- a shift left of supply
- a shift right of supply
! Own-price change → movement along.
@mcq Incomes rise and people buy more restaurant meals at every price. This is…
+ a rightward shift of demand
- a movement along demand
- a leftward shift of demand
- a supply shift
! Income is not on the axes → the curve shifts.
@mcq On a cost line C = F + c·Q, a rise in rent (part of F) is shown as…
+ a parallel upward shift of the line
- a movement along the line
- a steeper line | the slope is the per-unit cost c, which hasn't changed.
- no change
! Intercept rises, slope unchanged.

## F3.5 Types of charts and data
- **Time series**: one variable over time (line chart). Example: monthly sales.
- **Cross-section**: many units at one moment (bar chart). Example: GDP per
  person of EU countries in 2024.
- **Panel data**: many units over time.
- **Scatter plot**: two variables, one point per observation, to see
  relationships (unit F16).
- **Histogram**: distribution of one variable (unit F4).
- **Pie / stacked bars**: shares of a whole.

Choose the chart that answers the question: trends → lines; comparisons →
bars; relationships → scatter; distribution → histogram.

@mcq To show how a company's monthly revenue evolved over 3 years, use…
+ a line chart (time series)
- a pie chart | pies show shares at one moment.
- a scatter of two unrelated variables
- a histogram
! Trends over time → line chart.
@mcq Data on the unemployment rate of all 27 EU countries in 2024 is…
+ cross-sectional data
- time-series data | one moment, many units.
- panel data
- experimental data
! Many units, one period.
@mcq To see whether advertising spend and sales move together across 50 shops, use…
+ a scatter plot
- a pie chart
- a line chart over time
- a single bar
! Relationships between two variables → scatter.

## F3.6 Stocks and flows
- A **flow** is measured **per period**: income per year, sales per month,
  production per turn, cash flow.
- A **stock** is measured **at a moment**: wealth, inventory, debt, cash in
  the bank, population.

Flows change stocks: **stock_end = stock_start + inflows − outflows**.

> **Example.** Inventory at the start of the month 120 units; produced 300; sold
> 340. Inventory at the end = 120 + 300 − 340 = **80**.

Comparing a stock with a flow needs care: debt (stock) / GDP (flow per year)
is "years of GDP".

@num Opening cash €8,000; receipts in the month €15,000; payments €17,500. Closing cash (€)?
= 5500
! 8,000 + 15,000 − 17,500.
@num Opening stock 250 units, production 400, sales 520. Closing stock?
= 130
! 250 + 400 − 520.
@mcq Which is a STOCK?
+ the inventory held on 31 December
- monthly sales | per period → flow.
- annual GDP | per year → flow.
- weekly production
! Measured at a point in time.
@mcq Which is a FLOW?
+ revenue per quarter
- cash in the bank today
- national debt
- number of employees on 1 January
! Measured per period.

## F3.7 Misleading graphs
Common tricks (intentional or not):

- **Truncated axis**: the y-axis starts at 95 instead of 0, so a 2 % change
  looks enormous.
- **Different scales** on two charts placed side by side.
- **Nominal values over long periods** without adjusting for inflation.
- **Cherry-picked periods**: start the chart at a low point to show growth.
- **Areas or 3-D icons** that scale in two dimensions (a coin twice as tall
  looks four times bigger).

Defence: read the numbers on the axis, compute the % change yourself.

@mcq A bar chart of two values, 102 and 104, makes the second bar look twice as tall. Most likely…
+ the vertical axis starts near 100 instead of 0
- the data are wrong
- the second value is double the first
- it uses a log scale
! Truncated axis exaggerates a 2 % difference.
@num The chart's bars show 102 and 104. True percentage difference (%)?
= 1.96 % ~0.02
! (104 − 102)/102 = 1.96 %.
@mcq A chart of house prices since 1980 in nominal euros overstates the real increase because…
+ it doesn't remove general inflation
- house prices never change
- it uses a time series
- it starts in 1980
! Compare real values over long periods.

## F3.8 Logarithmic scales
On a **log scale**, equal vertical distances mean equal **ratios** (×2, ×10),
not equal differences. A series growing at a constant percentage rate appears
as a **straight line**.

Use log scales for long periods of growth (GDP over a century, stock indices,
company revenue from start-up to giant), and to compare growth rates of
series with very different sizes.

Useful facts: ln(1 + g) ≈ g for small g; the difference of logs ≈ the
percentage change: ln(110) − ln(100) = 0.0953 ≈ 10 %.

> **Example.** On a log axis, going from 100 to 1,000 takes the same space as
> going from 1,000 to 10,000.

@mcq A series that grows at a constant 5 % per year, plotted on a log scale, looks like…
+ a straight line
- an exponential curve | that's how it looks on a linear scale.
- a horizontal line
- a U shape
! Constant growth rate = constant slope in logs.
@num ln(105) − ln(100) ≈ ? (decimal, 3 places)
= 0.0488 ~0.001
! ln(1.05) = 0.0488 ≈ 5 %.
@mcq On a log scale, the distance from 10 to 100 equals the distance from…
+ 100 to 1,000
- 100 to 190 | equal distances are equal RATIOS.
- 0 to 10
- 50 to 100
! Both are ×10.

## F3.9 Reading tables
Tables pack many numbers; read them systematically:

1. Title, source, **units** (thousands? %?), period.
2. Row and column labels; are totals included?
3. Which **comparison** answers your question (rows over time? columns
   across groups?).
4. Compute shares, changes or ratios rather than staring at raw numbers.

> **Example.** Sales (€k): product A 2023 = 400, 2024 = 460; product B 2023 =
> 600, 2024 = 630. A grew 15 %, B grew 5 %. B is still larger, but A's **share**
> rose from 40 % to 460/1,090 = 42.2 %.

@num Sales (€k): A 300 → 360, B 700 → 740. Growth of total sales (%)?
= 10 %
! 1,000 → 1,100.
@num Same table: share of A in total sales in the second year (%)?
= 32.73 % ~0.05
! 360/1,100 = 32.73 %.
@num Same table: growth of B (%)?
= 5.71 % ~0.02
! 40/700 = 5.71 %.

## F3.10 From data to a question
Data analysis starts with a question, not with the data. A good business or
economic question is **specific**, **measurable** and tied to a **decision**:

- vague: "How are sales?"
- good: "Did the weekly units sold of product X change after we raised its price
  by 10 % in March, compared with similar products whose prices did not change?"

Then choose: which variable (outcome), which comparison (before/after, with/
without), which period, which possible confounders (season, competitors'
moves).

> **Example.** Measuring the effect of a promotion only with "sales during the
> promotion" ignores what sales would have been without it — always ask
> "compared with what?".

@mcq Which is the best analytical question?
+ Did weekly units sold change after the 10 % price rise, compared with similar products?
- How is the business doing?
- Is our price good?
- What do the data say?
! Specific outcome, comparison and decision.
@mcq A promotion week sold 500 units. To judge the promotion you also need…
+ what sales would have been without it (a comparison)
- nothing else
- the colour of the ads
- next year's sales
! "Compared with what?"
@mcq Sales rose after a price cut in December. The main confounder to worry about is…
+ seasonality (December is usually strong)
- the currency
- the logo
- nothing
! Other things changed at the same time.
