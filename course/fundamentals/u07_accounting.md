# F7 · Accounting foundations | Accounting & finance

## F7.1 Why accounting exists
Accounting records, classifies and reports a firm's economic events so that
people can make decisions: managers (what to produce), investors (should I
buy shares?), lenders (will I be repaid?), tax authorities.

- **Financial accounting** follows standards (IFRS, Italian OIC) and reports to
  outsiders.
- **Management accounting** serves internal decisions (costs, budgets).

The **entity principle**: the firm's accounts are separate from the owner's
personal money. The **going-concern** assumption: the firm will keep operating.
**Monetary unit**: everything is measured in money.

@mcq Which user relies mostly on MANAGEMENT accounting?
+ the production manager deciding which product to make
- a bank deciding a loan | outsiders use financial statements.
- the tax authority
- a shareholder
! Internal decision makers.
@mcq The owner pays her personal holiday from her own account. In the firm's books this is…
+ not recorded (entity principle)
- an expense of the firm
- a liability of the firm
- revenue
! Personal and business finances are separate.
@mcq The going-concern assumption means…
+ the firm is expected to continue operating
- the firm will be sold
- all assets are valued at liquidation prices
- the firm has no debts
! Basis for valuing assets at cost.

## F7.2 Assets
An **asset** is a resource controlled by the firm from which future economic
benefits are expected.

- **Current assets** (used within a year): cash, accounts receivable (customers
  owe you), inventory (raw materials, work in progress, finished goods),
  prepaid expenses.
- **Non-current assets**: property, plant and equipment, vehicles, patents,
  software, long-term investments.

Inventory is an asset because it is expected to turn into cash when sold — if
it can never be sold, it loses its value.

> **Example.** A shop owns €5,000 cash, €12,000 of goods on shelves, is owed
> €3,000 by a client and owns a €20,000 van → total assets €40,000.

@num Cash €7,000, inventory €15,000, receivables €4,000, equipment €24,000. Total assets (€)?
= 50000
! Sum.
@num Same firm: current assets (€)?
= 26000
! 7,000 + 15,000 + 4,000.
@mcq Which is NOT an asset?
+ a loan the firm must repay
- money customers owe the firm
- finished goods in the warehouse
- a delivery van
! A loan to repay is a liability.

## F7.3 Liabilities
A **liability** is a present obligation to transfer resources (usually cash)
to others.

- **Current liabilities**: accounts payable (suppliers you owe), short-term
  loans, wages and taxes due.
- **Non-current liabilities**: long-term bank loans, bonds issued.

Liabilities are claims of **outsiders** on the firm's assets.

> **Example.** The shop owes €2,500 to a supplier and €10,000 to a bank (5-year
> loan) → liabilities €12,500 (current €2,500, non-current €10,000).

@num Payables €6,000, short-term loan €4,000, 5-year bank loan €20,000. Current liabilities (€)?
= 10000
! 6,000 + 4,000.
@num Same: total liabilities (€)?
= 30000
! 10,000 + 20,000.
@mcq Buying goods from a supplier who will be paid in 30 days creates…
+ an account payable (liability)
- an account receivable | that's money owed TO you.
- revenue
- equity
! An obligation to pay.

## F7.4 Equity
**Equity** is the owners' residual claim: what is left of the assets after
paying all liabilities.

```
equity = assets − liabilities
```

It grows with **owners' contributions** (share capital) and **retained
profits**; it falls with losses and dividends paid to owners.

> **Example.** Assets €40,000, liabilities €12,500 → equity €27,500. If the
> firm earns €5,000 of profit and keeps it, equity becomes €32,500.

Negative equity means liabilities exceed assets — the firm is technically
insolvent.

@num Assets €80,000, liabilities €55,000. Equity (€)?
= 25000
! 80,000 − 55,000.
@num Equity €25,000; the year's profit is €6,000; dividends paid €2,000. Equity at year end (€)?
= 29000
! 25,000 + 6,000 − 2,000.
@mcq Equity falls when…
+ the firm makes a loss
- the firm borrows money | borrowing raises cash and a liability equally.
- owners invest more
- the firm earns a profit
! Losses reduce the owners' claim.

## F7.5 The accounting equation
```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

Every asset was financed either by creditors (liabilities) or by owners
(equity). The equation holds **after every transaction**: each event changes at
least two items so that both sides stay equal.

> **Example.** Owners invest €10,000 cash: assets +10,000 (cash) = equity
> +10,000. The firm borrows €5,000: assets +5,000 (cash) = liabilities +5,000.
> It buys €3,000 of stock with cash: cash −3,000, inventory +3,000 → totals
> unchanged. Balance: assets 15,000 = liabilities 5,000 + equity 10,000.

@num Liabilities €30,000, equity €45,000. Total assets (€)?
= 75000
! 30,000 + 45,000.
@num Assets €90,000, equity €35,000. Liabilities (€)?
= 55000
! 90,000 − 35,000.
@mcq Paying a supplier €1,000 that the firm owed…
+ reduces cash (asset) and payables (liability) by 1,000
- reduces cash and equity | it's settling a liability, not an expense.
- increases assets
- doesn't change anything
! Both sides fall equally.

## F7.6 Analysing transactions
For each transaction ask: **which items change, in which direction, by how
much?** Then check the equation still balances.

| transaction | assets | liabilities | equity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| owners invest 20,000 cash | +20,000 cash | | +20,000 |
| buy stock 4,000 on credit | +4,000 inventory | +4,000 payables | |
| sell stock costing 1,000 for 1,800 cash | +1,800 cash −1,000 inventory | | +800 (profit) |
| pay wages 500 | −500 cash | | −500 (expense) |

After these: assets 24,300 = liabilities 4,000 + equity 20,300.

= 12000

@num Same: equity now (€)?

@mcq Selling goods on credit (customer pays next month) increases…
+ receivables (asset) and equity via revenue

! A claim on the customer.

## F7.7 Revenues and expenses
**Revenue** increases equity by delivering goods or services; **expenses**
decrease equity by consuming resources to earn revenue (cost of goods sold,
wages, rent, depreciation, interest).

```
profit = revenues − expenses        → added to equity (retained earnings)
```

Not every cash outflow is an expense: buying inventory or equipment converts
cash into another asset; repaying a loan reduces a liability. Not every cash
inflow is revenue: borrowing or receiving owners' capital is financing.

@mcq Which is an expense?
+ wages paid to staff for this month's work
- repayment of a bank loan | that reduces a liability.
- purchase of a delivery van | that's an asset.
- owners' capital
! Consumes resources to earn revenue.
@mcq Which cash inflow is NOT revenue?
+ a new bank loan
- cash sales to customers
- fees for a service delivered
- interest earned on deposits
! Borrowing is financing.
@num Revenues €70,000; cost of goods sold €40,000; wages €15,000; rent €6,000. Profit (€)?
= 9000
! 70,000 − 61,000.

## F7.8 Debits and credits
Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction in at least two accounts
with equal **debit** (left, Dr) and **credit** (right, Cr) amounts.

| account type | increase with | decrease with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| assets | debit | credit |
| expenses | debit | credit |
| liabilities | credit | debit |
| equity | credit | debit |
| revenues | credit | debit |

Mnemonic **DEA–LER**: Debits increase Expenses and Assets; Liabilities, Equity
and Revenues increase with credits. Total debits always equal total credits.

> **Example.** Buy stock €2,000 on credit: Dr Inventory 2,000 / Cr Payables 2,000.

@mcq Receiving €5,000 of cash from a bank loan is recorded as…
+ Dr Cash 5,000 / Cr Loan 5,000
- Dr Loan / Cr Cash | that would be a repayment.
- Dr Cash / Cr Revenue | a loan is not revenue.
- Dr Equity / Cr Cash
! Asset up (debit), liability up (credit).
@mcq Paying rent of €1,200 in cash is…
+ Dr Rent expense / Cr Cash
- Dr Cash / Cr Rent expense
- Dr Rent expense / Cr Payables | it was paid in cash now.
- Dr Equity / Cr Rent
! Expense up (debit), asset down (credit).
@mcq Revenues increase with…
+ credits
- debits
- either
- neither
! DEA–LER.

## F7.9 T-accounts and balances
A **T-account** shows one account's debits (left) and credits (right). Its
**balance** = difference between the two sides, on the larger side.

```
          Cash
  Dr                 Cr
  10,000 (capital)   3,000 (stock)
   5,000 (sales)       500 (wages)
  --------          --------
  15,000             3,500      → debit balance 11,500
```

Assets and expenses normally have **debit** balances; liabilities, equity and
revenues **credit** balances.

@num Cash T-account: debits 10,000, 6,500, 2,000; credits 4,000, 3,200. Balance (€)?
= 11300
! 18,500 − 7,200 = 11,300 debit.
@num Payables T-account: credits 4,000 and 2,500; debit 3,000. Balance (€)?
= 3500
! 6,500 − 3,000 = 3,500 credit.
@mcq A liability account normally has…
+ a credit balance
- a debit balance
- no balance
- a negative balance
! Liabilities increase with credits.

## F7.10 Journal entries
The **journal** records transactions in date order, each as an entry: date,
accounts debited, accounts credited, amounts, short description. Entries are
then **posted** to the ledger (the set of T-accounts).

```
Mar 3  Dr Inventory        2,400
          Cr Cash                 2,400   bought components
Mar 9  Dr Cash             3,000
          Cr Sales revenue        3,000   sold 10 units
       Dr Cost of goods sold 1,600
          Cr Inventory            1,600   cost of the units sold
```

A sale records two things: the revenue (at selling price) and the cost of the
goods leaving inventory (at cost).

@num Using the entries above, inventory balance after Mar 9 (€)?
= 800
! 2,400 − 1,600.
@num Same entries: gross profit on the sale (€)?
= 1400
! 3,000 − 1,600.
@mcq When goods costing 1,600 are sold for 3,000, inventory is reduced by…
+ 1,600 (cost)
- 3,000 (price) | inventory is carried at cost.
- 1,400
- 0
! The cost leaves inventory and becomes COGS.

## F7.11 The trial balance
A **trial balance** lists every account's balance: debit balances in one
column, credit balances in the other. The two totals must be equal — if not,
an error was made (a one-sided posting, wrong amount).

Equal totals don't prove correctness: an entry posted to the wrong account
with the right amount still balances.

> **Example.** Cash 11,500 Dr; Inventory 3,000 Dr; Equipment 8,000 Dr; Payables
> 2,500 Cr; Loan 5,000 Cr; Capital 12,000 Cr; Revenue 6,000 Cr; Expenses 3,000
> Dr. Debits 25,500 = credits 25,500.

@num Debit balances: cash 4,000, inventory 6,000, expenses 2,000. Credit balances: payables 3,000, capital 7,000, revenue X. What is X if the trial balance balances?
= 2000
! 12,000 = 10,000 + X.
@mcq A trial balance that balances guarantees…
+ total debits equal total credits — not that every entry is right
- that there are no errors | wrong-account postings still balance.
- that the firm is profitable
- that cash is positive
! It checks arithmetic equality only.
@mcq An entry with debit 500 but credit 50 causes…
+ a trial balance that doesn't balance
- no visible error
- higher profit
- a liability
! One-sided error.

## F7.12 Accrual vs cash basis
- **Cash basis**: record revenue when cash is received and expenses when paid.
- **Accrual basis** (required for companies): record revenue when **earned**
  (goods delivered) and expenses when **incurred** (resources used), matching
  costs to the revenues they generate.

> **Example.** December: goods delivered for €8,000, customer pays in January.
> Accrual: December revenue 8,000 (receivable). Cash basis: January revenue.

Accrual profit and cash flow differ — the cash flow statement (F10) reconciles
them.

@mcq Under accrual accounting, a sale delivered in March and paid in May is revenue in…
+ March
- May | that's the cash basis.
- April
- split
! Revenue when earned.
@num In June a firm delivers goods for €12,000 (half paid in June, half in July) and pays wages of €4,000 for June in July. June profit on an accrual basis (€)?
= 8000
x 6000 | accrual counts the whole sale and the June wages, regardless of payment dates.
! 12,000 − 4,000.
@num Same firm: June net cash flow (€)?
= 6000
! Only the half paid in June; wages paid in July.
