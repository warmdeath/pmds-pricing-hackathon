# F1 · Thinking like an economist | Reasoning

## F1.1 What economics studies
Economics studies **how people, firms and governments choose when resources are
limited**. It is not "the study of money": money is only one resource. Time,
attention, land, workers, machines and raw materials are scarce too.

Two branches:

- **Microeconomics** — individual decisions and single markets (a consumer
  choosing a phone, a firm setting a price, the market for coffee).
- **Macroeconomics** — the whole economy (GDP, inflation, unemployment,
  interest rates).

Economists ask three kinds of questions: **what happens** (description), **why
it happens** (explanation, via models) and **what would happen if** (prediction
and policy). Every economic question hides a **choice under scarcity**.

> **Example.** A city has one free square: market, car park or playground? There
> is no right answer without asking who gains, who loses and what each use is
> worth — that is an economic question even though no money changes hands.

## F1.2 Scarcity and trade-offs
**Scarcity** means wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them. It is
not the same as poverty: a billionaire still has only 24 hours a day.

Because of scarcity, every choice is a **trade-off**: getting more of one thing
means getting less of something else. Firms trade off quality vs cost, speed vs
accuracy, stock vs cash. Governments trade off spending on schools vs hospitals
vs lower taxes.

A useful habit: whenever someone proposes "more X", ask **"instead of what?"**.

> **Example.** A shop has €10,000 of cash. Spending €6,000 on new stock leaves
> €4,000 for advertising, rent or a safety buffer. The €6,000 of stock is not
> "free" because the shop already had the cash.

Trade-offs exist even when something is "free": a free concert costs the
evening you could have spent elsewhere.

@mcq A billionaire faces scarcity because…
+ time (and many other resources) are still limited
- she can buy anything | money doesn't create more hours.
- scarcity only concerns poor people | scarcity ≠ poverty.
- of taxes
! Scarcity is about limited resources relative to wants, for everyone.

= 18000
! 50,000 − 32,000 = 18,000: every euro spent on materials is a euro not available elsewhere.

+ the visit still uses scarce time that has other uses
- museums are never free
- nothing, it really costs nothing | time has value.
- it ignores inflation
! Free in money is not free in opportunity.

## F1.3 Opportunity cost
The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the **best alternative you
give up**. Only the best one — you could only have done one thing instead.

It includes money you pay (**explicit cost**) and value you forgo without paying
(**implicit cost**), such as your time or the interest your cash could earn.

> **Example.** You choose a summer course costing €1,000 instead of a summer job
> paying €3,000 (your best alternative). Opportunity cost = €1,000 + €3,000 =
> **€4,000**.

> **Example.** Cash of €20,000 sitting in a firm could earn 3 % per year in a
> deposit. Keeping it idle for a year has an implicit cost of €600.

Opportunity cost is the economist's **true** cost. A choice is worth making only
if its benefit exceeds its opportunity cost.

@num You can work a Saturday shift for €90, tutor for €70, or go to a party. If you go to the party, what is the opportunity cost (in €)?
= 90
x 160 | only the BEST alternative counts, not the sum of all alternatives.
x 70 | that's the second-best alternative.
! The best alternative forgone is the €90 shift.
@num A course costs €1,500 in fees. Instead you could have worked and earned €2,500. Opportunity cost of the course (€)?
= 4000
x 1500 | you forgot the implicit cost: the earnings you give up.
! Explicit 1,500 + implicit 2,500 = 4,000.
@num A firm keeps €40,000 idle for a year; it could earn 2.5 % in a deposit. Implicit annual cost (€)?
= 1000
! 40,000 × 0.025 = 1,000.

+ the value of the best alternative forgone
- the sum of all alternatives forgone | you can only do one alternative instead.
- the money paid | that's only the explicit part.
- the average cost
! Best alternative only, explicit + implicit.

## F1.4 Explicit vs implicit costs, accounting vs economic profit
- **Explicit costs**: payments to others (wages, rent, materials).
- **Implicit costs**: opportunity costs of resources you own (your own time,
  your own capital, your own building).

Two notions of profit follow:

```
accounting profit = revenue − explicit costs
economic profit   = revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs
```

Economic profit tells you whether the activity beats your best alternative.
Zero economic profit is fine: it means you earn exactly what you'd earn
elsewhere ("normal profit").

> **Example.** A bakery owner: revenue €200,000, explicit costs €150,000 →
> accounting profit €50,000. She could earn €45,000 as an employee and €3,000 of
> interest on the €100,000 she invested → implicit costs €48,000. Economic
> profit = €2,000: the bakery is (just) worth it.

@num Revenue €120,000; explicit costs €90,000. Accounting profit (€)?
= 30000
! 120,000 − 90,000.
@num Same business; the owner could earn €35,000 elsewhere. Economic profit (€)?
= -5000
x 30000 | that's accounting profit; subtract the implicit cost.
! 30,000 − 35,000 = −5,000: the owner would be better off working elsewhere.
@mcq Zero economic profit means…
+ the owner earns exactly as much as in the best alternative
- the business makes no money at all | accounting profit can be positive.
- the business should close immediately
- taxes are too high
! "Normal profit": the resources earn their opportunity cost.

## F1.5 Incentives
An **incentive** is anything that changes the costs or benefits of a choice.
People and firms respond to incentives — usually in the expected direction,
sometimes in surprising ways.

- Higher price of petrol → people drive less, buy efficient cars.
- A bonus per unit sold → salespeople push volume (maybe with big discounts).
- A tax on plastic bags → fewer bags used.

**Unintended consequences** happen when incentives push in directions nobody
planned. Classic case: paying a bounty per dead cobra led people to *breed*
cobras. Good analysis asks: *who* is affected by this rule, and how will
they adjust?

> **Example.** A shop pays staff by revenue, not profit. Staff may sell at any
> discount that closes a deal — revenue rises, profit may fall.

@mcq A city charges a congestion fee to enter the centre. The most likely effect is…

- no effect, people must drive | some trips can change time, mode or route.
- traffic rises elsewhere only
! A higher cost of driving in → less driving in (an incentive at work).

+ they may give large discounts that raise revenue but cut profit

- none
! Incentives aligned with revenue, not profit.

+ unintended consequences of incentives

## F1.6 Thinking at the margin
"Marginal" means **one more** (or one less). Rational decisions compare the
**marginal benefit (MB)** with the **marginal cost (MC)** of the next unit:

- MB > MC → do one more.
- MB < MC → stop (or do one less).

Averages mislead. A restaurant with an average cost of €15 per meal should
still accept a last-minute group paying €10 per meal if each extra meal only
costs €6 in ingredients (the rent and staff are paid anyway).

> **Example.** Hours of study for an exam: the 1st hour raises your grade a lot,
> the 10th hour very little. Stop when the extra grade is no longer worth the
> extra hour.

@num A product sells for €8. The marginal costs of units 1–5 are €3, €5, €7, €9, €11. How many units should be produced?
= 3 ~0.01
x 5 | units 4 and 5 cost more than they earn.
! Produce while MC ≤ 8: units 1–3.
@num Average cost per meal is €15, ingredients for one extra meal cost €6. A group offers €10 per meal for 20 extra meals (spare capacity). Gain from accepting (€)?
= 80
x -100 | average cost includes fixed costs that don't change.
! (10 − 6) × 20 = 80.
@mcq Which comparison should drive "one more unit?" decisions?
+ marginal benefit vs marginal cost
- total benefit vs total cost
- average benefit vs average cost | averages include units already decided.
- price vs average cost
! Marginal thinking.

## F1.7 Sunk costs
A **sunk cost** has already been paid and **cannot be recovered**, whatever you
do next. Because no future choice changes it, it should **not** affect future
choices.

The **sunk cost fallacy**: continuing a project because "we already spent so
much". The right question is always forward-looking: *from today on*, do the
extra benefits exceed the extra costs?

Careful: not every past payment is sunk. If you can resell an asset, its resale
value is still available — that part is not sunk.

> **Example.** A firm spent €2 m on a product. Finishing costs another €1 m;
> expected revenue is €1.5 m. Finish it: +€0.5 m from here. If expected revenue
> were €0.8 m, stop — even though €2 m "would be wasted". It's gone either way.

@num A project already cost €500k (unrecoverable). Finishing it costs €300k more and will bring €400k. Net gain from finishing, from today (€k)?
= 100
x -400 | the €500k is sunk; it's spent whichever way you decide.
! 400 − 300 = +100k → finish.
@num A machine bought for €50,000 can be resold today for €20,000. What part of its price is sunk (€)?
= 30000
x 50000 | the resale value can still be recovered.
! 50,000 − 20,000 = 30,000 is unrecoverable.
@mcq "We can't stop now, we've already invested so much." This is…
+ the sunk cost fallacy
- marginal thinking
- opportunity cost reasoning
- correct reasoning
! Past unrecoverable spending is irrelevant to the forward decision.

## F1.8 Positive vs normative statements
- **Positive** statements describe or predict: they can be checked against
  data. "Raising the minimum wage reduces employment of teenagers."
- **Normative** statements express values: what *ought* to be. "The minimum
  wage should be raised."

Economists disagree for two reasons: different **evidence/models** (positive
disagreement) and different **values** (normative disagreement). Separating the
two makes debates more productive.

Clue words for normative: *should, ought, fair, better, too high*.

> **Example.** "A tax on sugary drinks reduces their consumption by 10 %" is
> positive (testable). "Sugary drinks should be taxed" is normative.

@mcq Which statement is positive?
+ An increase in tobacco taxes reduces smoking among young people.
- Tobacco should be banned. | "should" signals a value judgement.
- Taxes on tobacco are unfair to the poor. | "unfair" is a value judgement.
- The government ought to help smokers quit.
! It can be tested with data.
@mcq Which statement is normative?
+ Rich people should pay a higher share of their income in tax.
- Higher taxes reduce hours worked by some workers.
- Inflation in Italy was above 8 % in 2022.
- When prices rise, people buy less.
! "Should" → value judgement.
@mcq Two economists agree on all the evidence but disagree on a policy. Their disagreement is most likely…
+ normative (about values)
- positive (about facts)
- about mathematics
- impossible
! Same facts, different values.

## F1.9 Models and "other things equal"
A **model** is a deliberate simplification that captures the essential
mechanism. Like a map: a subway map is useless for walking distances but
perfect for changing trains.

Models rely on **assumptions** ("two goods", "firms maximise profit"). A good
model is judged by whether it explains and predicts well, not by whether its
assumptions are literally true.

**Ceteris paribus** ("other things equal"): change one variable at a time and
hold the rest fixed, to isolate an effect. Real data rarely hold other things
equal — which is why careful evidence (lesson F1.10, unit F16) is needed.

> **Example.** "If the price of pizza rises, people buy fewer pizzas" holds
> ceteris paribus. If at the same time incomes rise, pizza sales might go up —
> not because the price rule is wrong, but because something else changed.

@mcq The main purpose of an economic model is to…
+ simplify reality to isolate the key mechanism
- describe every detail of reality | that would be as complex as reality itself.
- prove that markets are always efficient
- replace data
! Like a map: useful because it leaves things out.
@mcq Pizza prices rose AND pizza sales rose in the same year. Most likely explanation consistent with the law of demand?
+ something else changed too (e.g. incomes or tastes), so ceteris paribus doesn't hold
- the law of demand is false
- people like paying more
- data are wrong
! Other things were not equal.
@mcq "Ceteris paribus" means…
+ other things held constant
- in the long run
- on average
- with certainty
! Latin for "other things being equal".

## F1.10 Correlation is not causation
Two variables are **correlated** when they move together. That does not mean
one **causes** the other. Three traps:

1. **Common cause**: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer (heat).
2. **Reverse causality**: cities with more police have more crime — because
   crime attracts police, not the opposite.
3. **Coincidence**: with enough data series, some will move together by chance.

Establishing causation needs **experiments** (randomised trials, A/B tests) or
careful **natural experiments**. Firms use A/B tests: randomly show half the
customers price A and half price B, then compare.

> **Example.** Stores that advertise more have higher sales. Does advertising
> cause sales, or do stores with high sales have more money to advertise? Both
> may be true.

@mcq Ice-cream sales and sunburns are positively correlated. The best explanation is…
