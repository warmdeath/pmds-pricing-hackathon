## F18.1 Case: the café's opening budget
A student opens a café. Fixed costs €6,000/month (rent, staff); coffee sells at
€2.20, variable cost €0.70; pastries €3.00, variable cost €1.50. Expected mix:
70 % coffee, 30 % pastries (by items sold).

Combine F6 (contribution, break-even) with F4 (weighted averages):
weighted contribution per item = 0.7 × 1.50 + 0.3 × 1.50 = €1.50; break-even =
6,000/1.50 = 4,000 items per month, about 154 per day over 26 days.

Questions a manager asks next: is 154 items/day realistic (data on foot
traffic)? How sensitive is profit to the mix and to the coffee price?

@num Café: fixed costs €6,000/month; every item (coffee or pastry) contributes €1.50. Break-even items per month?
= 4000
! 6,000/1.50.
@num Café: fixed costs €6,000/month; each item contributes €1.50; expected sales 5,200 items/month. Monthly profit (€)?
= 1800
! 1,200 × 1.50.
@num Café: break-even 4,000 items/month; expected sales 5,200 items. Margin of safety (%)?
= 23.08 % ~0.05
! 1,200/5,200.

## F18.2 Case: pricing a new product
A firm can charge €40, €45 or €50 for a gadget with variable cost €22 and fixed
costs €20,000. Market tests estimate demand: 2,000, 1,700, 1,300 units.

Profits: €40 → 18 × 2,000 − 20,000 = 16,000; €45 → 23 × 1,700 − 20,000 =
19,100; €50 → 28 × 1,300 − 20,000 = 16,400. Best: **€45**.

Arc elasticity between €40 and €45 = (−300/1,850)/(5/42.5) = −1.38 (elastic),
yet raising the price still increased profit — because costs fall with fewer
units. Revenue and profit are different objectives.

@num Gadget: variable cost €22, fixed costs €20,000; at €45 the firm sells 1,700 units. Profit at €45 (€)?
= 19100
! 23 × 1,700 − 20,000.
@num At €40 a gadget sells 2,000 units; at €45 it sells 1,700. Revenue at €40 minus revenue at €45 (€)?
= 3500
! 80,000 − 76,500.
@num At €45 a gadget sells 1,700 units; at €50 it sells 1,300. Arc (midpoint) elasticity between the two prices?
= -2.53 ~0.01
! (−400/1,500)/(5/47.5) = −0.2667/0.1053 = −2.53.

## F18.3 Case: should we accept the large order?
A factory runs at 80 % of a 10,000-unit capacity, selling at €50 with variable
cost €30. A retailer offers to buy 3,000 units at €38.

- Spare capacity = 2,000 units. Accepting 3,000 means giving up 1,000 regular
  sales (lost contribution €20 each).
- Gain on 3,000 special units: (38 − 30) × 3,000 = €24,000.
- Lost regular contribution: 1,000 × 20 = €20,000.
- Net: **+€4,000** — before considering whether regular customers will demand
  the lower price too.

@num Factory: capacity 10,000 units, using 8,000; regular price €50, variable cost €30. A one-off order of 3,000 units at €38 would displace 1,000 regular sales. Net gain from accepting (€)?
= 4000
! 24,000 − 20,000.
@num Factory: capacity 10,000 units, using 8,000; regular price €50, variable cost €30. A one-off order of 3,000 units at €35 would displace 1,000 regular sales. Net gain from accepting (€)?
= -5000
! 5 × 3,000 − 20,000.
@num Capacity 10,000, using 8,000; regular price €50, variable cost €30. Minimum price per unit at which a 3,000-unit one-off order breaks even (€)?

## F18.4 Case: cash runway
A start-up has €180,000 cash. Monthly fixed costs €40,000; it sells 500 units a
month with contribution €50 each, growing 10 % per month.

Month 1 net cash flow = 25,000 − 40,000 = −15,000. Contribution needed to break
even: 40,000/50 = 800 units. Units grow 500 → 550 → 605 → 665.5 → 732 → 805:
break-even in month 6. Cumulative burn until then ≈ 15,000 + 12,500 + 9,750 +
6,725 + 3,398 = €47,373 → cash is sufficient.

Profit can be the right idea and the firm can still die if cash runs out first.

@num Start-up: fixed costs €40,000/month, contribution €50 per unit. Units needed per month to break even?
= 800
! 40,000/50.
@num Start-up: fixed costs €40,000/month, contribution €50 per unit, 500 units sold in month 1. Month-1 net cash flow (€)?
= -15000
! 500 × 50 − 40,000.
@num Start-up with €180,000 cash loses €15,000 a month (500 units × €50 − €40,000). Months of runway?
= 12
! 180,000/15,000.

## F18.6 Case: investing in a machine
A machine costs €60,000, saves €18,000 per year for 5 years, no residual value.
Cost of capital 8 %.

NPV = −60,000 + 18,000 × annuity factor(8 %, 5) = −60,000 + 18,000 × 3.9927 =
**€11,869**. Payback = 3.33 years. IRR ≈ 15.2 %.

Depreciation (straight-line) is €12,000/year: it affects accounting profit, not
the cash savings — NPV uses cash flows.

## F18.7 Case: loan or leasing?
Option 1: borrow €30,000 at 6 % for 3 years (annual payments). Option 2: lease
for €11,500 per year (end of year), 3 years. Compare costs in PV at 6 %.

Loan payment = 30,000 × 0.06/(1 − 1.06⁻³) = €11,223.29; PV of loan payments at
6 % = 30,000. PV of lease = 11,500 × 2.6730 = €30,740. Buying with the loan is
cheaper by about €740 in today's money (ignoring maintenance, taxes and residual
value).

@num Borrow €30,000 at 6 % for 3 years, equal annual payments. Annual payment (€)?

@num Lease: €11,500 at the end of each year for 3 years. PV at 6 % (€)?
= 30739.6 ~2
! 11,500 × 2.67301.
@num €30,000 loan at 6 % for 3 years, annual payments of €11,223.29. Total interest paid (€)?
= 3669.87 ~0.2

## F18.8 Case: inventory for a seasonal product
A shop orders winter jackets once. Cost €60, price €100, unsold jackets are
sold to an outlet at €30. Demand ~ N(500, 100²).

c_u = 100 − 60 = 40; c_o = 60 − 30 = 30; critical ratio 40/70 = 0.571;
z ≈ 0.18 → order ≈ 500 + 0.18 × 100 = **518**.

If unsold jackets were worth nothing (c_o = 60): ratio 0.4, z ≈ −0.25 → order
≈ 475. The value of leftovers changes the optimal stock.

@num Jackets: cost €60, price €100, unsold ones sold to an outlet at €30. Newsvendor critical ratio?
= 0.5714 ~0.001 %
! 40/70.
@num Jackets: cost €60, price €100, unsold ones worth €0. Newsvendor critical ratio?
= 0.4 %
! 40/100.
@num Demand ~ N(500, 100²) and the critical ratio is 0.5. Optimal order quantity?
= 500
! The median of N(500, 100²).

## F18.9 Case: did the campaign work?
Before an online campaign, daily orders averaged 200 (SD 30) over 60 days. In
the 25 campaign days the average was 214. Same weeks last year rose by 3 % from
the preceding period (seasonality).

- Seasonally expected: 206. Excess: 8 orders/day.
- SE of a 25-day mean ≈ 30/5 = 6 → z = 8/6 = 1.33 → two-sided p ≈ 0.18: not
  convincing evidence.
- Raw comparison (214 vs 200, z = 2.33) would have overstated the effect.

@num Daily orders: SD 30; 25 campaign days averaged 214 vs a seasonally expected 206. z statistic?
= 1.33 ~0.01
! 8/6.
@num Daily orders: SD 30; 25 campaign days averaged 214 vs a pre-campaign average of 200. z statistic?
= 2.33 ~0.01
! 14/6.
@mcq Orders rose from 200 to 214 a day during a campaign, but the same weeks last year also rose about 3 %. The main lesson is…
+ compare with the right baseline (seasonality) before judging
- campaigns never work
- z-tests are useless
- always use raw numbers
! Compared with what?

## F18.10 Case: risk of a supplier failure
A firm buys a key component from one supplier with a 5 % chance of a
disruption each quarter. A disruption costs €200,000. A second supplier
(independent) would cost an extra €4,000 per quarter; with two suppliers, the
loss happens only if both fail (and then costs the same).

- Single supplier expected loss per quarter = 0.05 × 200,000 = €10,000.
- Dual: P(both fail) = 0.0025 → expected loss €500 + €4,000 = €4,500.
- Dual sourcing saves €5,500 per quarter in expectation — **if** failures are
  independent. A common cause (same region, same port) breaks the calculation.

@num One supplier, 5 % chance of a disruption per quarter, each costing €200,000. Expected loss per quarter (€)?
= 10000
! 0.05 × 200,000.
@num Two independent suppliers, each 5 % likely to fail per quarter; a loss of €200,000 happens only if both fail; the second supplier costs €4,000 a quarter. Expected cost per quarter (€)?
= 4500
! 0.0025 × 200,000 + 4,000.
@num A supplier fails with probability 5 % each quarter, independently. P(at least one disruption in 4 quarters) (%)?
= 18.55 % ~0.05
! 1 − 0.95⁴.

## F18.11 Case: estimating demand from data
A shop tested five prices over comparable weeks: (€10, 100), (€12, 90), (€14,
76), (€16, 68), (€18, 56) units.

Regression: x̄ = 14, ȳ = 78; deviations of x: −4, −2, 0, 2, 4; of y: 22, 12, −2,
−10, −22. Sum of products = −220 → cov = −55; sum of squares of x = 40 →
var(x) = 10 → b = −5.5; a = 78 + 5.5 × 14 = 155 → Q̂ = 155 − 5.5P.

With variable cost €6: profit (P − 6)(155 − 5.5P) is maximised where
155 − 11P + 33 = 0 ⇒ P ≈ €17.09 — inside the tested range (€10–€18), so the
linear fit is a reasonable guide there; outside it would be extrapolation.

@num Price tests (price €, units): (10, 100), (12, 90), (14, 76), (16, 68), (18, 56). Least-squares slope of units on price?
= -5.5
! −220/40.
@num Fitted demand Q̂ = 155 − 5.5P. Predicted units at €15?
= 72.5 ~0.05
! 155 − 82.5.
@num Fitted demand Q̂ = 155 − 5.5P, variable cost €6. Profit-maximising price (€)?
= 17.09 ~0.01
! (155 + 33)/11 = 17.09.

## F18.12 Case: writing the recommendation
A recommendation memo combines everything:

1. **Question**: what decision?
2. **Answer first**: the recommendation and expected impact (± uncertainty).
3. **Evidence**: key numbers, sources, comparison baseline.
4. **Economics**: relevant costs, opportunity costs, incentives, competitors'
   likely reactions.
5. **Risks and sensitivity**: what would change the answer.
6. **Next steps**: what to test or monitor.

> **Example.** "Raise the price of product X from €45 to €48. Expected profit
> +€6k/month (range +€2k to +€9k). Based on a 6-week test vs a control group.
> Main risk: competitor undercutting; we will monitor weekly share and revert
> if units fall more than 12 %."
