# F10 · Financial statements | Accounting & finance

## F10.1 The three statements
A company reports three linked statements:

- **Balance sheet** (stato patrimoniale): assets, liabilities, equity **at a
  date** — a photograph.
- **Income statement** (conto economico): revenues and expenses **over a
  period** — a film of performance.
- **Cash flow statement** (rendiconto finanziario): where cash came from and
  went **over a period**.

Links: profit flows into equity (retained earnings); the change in cash on the
cash flow statement equals the change in the balance-sheet cash line.

@mcq Which statement shows the firm's situation at a single date?
+ balance sheet
- income statement | it covers a period.
- cash flow statement
- notes
! A stock, not a flow.
@mcq Net profit for the year ends up on the balance sheet in…
+ equity (retained earnings)
- liabilities
- cash, always | profit is not cash.
- inventory
! Profit retained increases equity.
@mcq The cash flow statement explains…
+ the change in the cash balance over the period
- the profit
- total assets
- the share price
! Opening cash + net cash flow = closing cash.

## F10.2 Balance sheet layout
Typical classification:

```
ASSETS                         EQUITY AND LIABILITIES
Non-current assets              Equity
  property, plant, equipment      share capital
  intangibles                     retained earnings
Current assets                  Non-current liabilities
  inventory                       long-term loans
  receivables                   Current liabilities
  cash                            payables, short-term debt
TOTAL ASSETS          =         TOTAL EQUITY + LIABILITIES
```

**Working capital** = current assets − current liabilities: the cushion
available to run daily operations.

@num Inventory 30, receivables 20, cash 10, payables 25, short-term debt 15. Working capital?
= 20
! 60 − 40.
@num PP&E 200, current assets 100, equity 150, long-term loans 90. Current liabilities?
= 60
! 300 − 150 − 90.
@mcq Negative working capital means…
+ current liabilities exceed current assets
- the firm is bankrupt | some businesses (supermarkets) run with it.
- profit is negative
- equity is negative
! A liquidity indicator, not a verdict.

## F10.3 Income statement layout
```
Revenue
− Cost of goods sold (COGS)
= Gross profit
− Operating expenses (salaries, rent, marketing, depreciation)
= Operating profit (EBIT)
− Interest expense
= Profit before tax
− Income tax
= Net profit
```

**EBITDA** = EBIT + depreciation & amortisation.

> **Example.** Revenue 500, COGS 300, opex 120 (incl. depreciation 20), interest
> 10, tax 25% → gross 200, EBIT 80, EBITDA 100, PBT 70, tax 17.5, net 52.5.

@num Revenue 800, COGS 480, operating expenses 200, interest 20, tax rate 25 %. Net profit?
= 75
! EBIT 120, PBT 100, tax 25.
@num Revenue 800, COGS 480. Gross margin (%)?
= 40 %
! 320/800.
@num EBIT 150, depreciation 40. EBITDA?
= 190
! 150 + 40.

## F10.4 Revenue recognition and matching
- Revenue is recognised when the firm **satisfies its obligation**: goods
  delivered, service performed — not when cash arrives, not when the order is
  signed.
- **Matching**: expenses are recorded in the period of the revenue they help
  generate. Cost of goods enters the income statement as **COGS when the goods
  are sold**, not when bought.

> **Example.** In Q1 a firm buys 1,000 units at €20, sells 700 at €35.
> Revenue 24,500; COGS 700 × 20 = 14,000; gross profit 10,500. The other 300
> units (€6,000) stay on the balance sheet as inventory.

@num Bought 500 units at €12, sold 350 at €20. Gross profit (€)?
= 2800
x 1000 | match costs to the units SOLD; unsold units stay in inventory.
! 7,000 − 4,200.
@num Bought 500 units at €12, sold 350 at €20. Value of ending inventory (€)?
= 1800
! 150 × 12.
@mcq A customer pays in advance for goods delivered next quarter. This quarter the firm records…
+ cash and a liability (deferred revenue), not revenue
- revenue
- an expense
- nothing
! Revenue when the obligation is satisfied.

## F10.5 Depreciation
Long-lived assets (machines, vehicles) are used over several years, so their
cost is spread as **depreciation** expense.

```
straight-line depreciation per year = (cost − residual value) / useful life
book value = cost − accumulated depreciation
```

Depreciation is a **non-cash** expense: the cash left when the asset was bought.

> **Example.** Machine €50,000, residual €5,000, life 5 years → €9,000 a year.
> After 2 years book value = 50,000 − 18,000 = €32,000.

@num Van €30,000, residual €6,000, life 4 years. Annual straight-line depreciation (€)?
= 6000
! 24,000/4.
@num Van €30,000, residual €6,000, life 4 years, straight-line. Book value after 3 years (€)?
= 12000
! 30,000 − 18,000.
@mcq Depreciation reduces profit but…
+ doesn't reduce cash in the year it is recorded
- reduces cash too | the cash was paid at purchase.
- increases taxes
- increases equity
! Non-cash expense.

## F10.6 Inventory costing: FIFO and weighted average
When identical units were bought at different prices, the cost assigned to
units sold depends on the method:

- **FIFO** (first in, first out): oldest costs go to COGS first.
- **Weighted average**: every unit carries the average cost.
- (LIFO: newest costs first — not allowed under IFRS.)

> **Example.** Buy 100 @ €10 then 100 @ €14; sell 120. FIFO COGS = 1,000 + 20
> × 14 = 1,280; ending inventory 80 × 14 = 1,120. Average cost = 12 → COGS =
> 1,440; inventory 960.

With rising prices FIFO gives lower COGS and higher profit.

@num Buy 50 @ €8 then 50 @ €12; sell 70. COGS under FIFO (€)?
= 640
! 50×8 + 20×12.
@num Buy 50 @ €8 then 50 @ €12; sell 70. COGS under weighted average (€)?
= 700
! average 10 × 70.
@num Buy 50 @ €8 then 50 @ €12; sell 70. Ending inventory under FIFO (€)?
= 360
! 30 × 12.

## F10.7 Inventory valuation and write-downs
Inventory is carried at the **lower of cost and net realisable value** (NRV =
expected selling price − costs to sell). If goods become obsolete or can only be
sold below cost, they are **written down** — the loss hits profit now.

Inventory that will never be sold is worth **zero**, whatever it cost: holding
it doesn't preserve value.

> **Example.** 200 units cost €30 each; they can now be sold only at €22 with
> €2 selling cost → NRV €20. Write-down = 200 × 10 = **€2,000**.

@num 100 units cost €50 each; expected selling price €45, selling costs €5. Write-down (€)?
= 1000
! NRV 40 → 100 × 10.
@num 300 units at cost €15, NRV €18. Carrying value (€)?
= 4500
! Lower of cost and NRV: cost 15.
@mcq Obsolete stock that can't be sold should be valued at…
+ zero
- its cost
- its original selling price
- the average cost
! No future benefit.

## F10.8 The cash flow statement
Three sections:

1. **Operating activities**: cash from running the business.
2. **Investing activities**: buying/selling long-term assets.
3. **Financing activities**: borrowing, repaying, issuing shares, dividends.

Opening cash + operating + investing + financing = closing cash.

> **Example.** Operating +120, investing −80 (new machines), financing −30
> (loan repayment) → cash +10.

**Free cash flow** = operating cash flow − capital expenditure: what is
available to owners and lenders.

@num Operating +200, investing −150, financing +30, opening cash 50. Closing cash?
= 130
! 50 + 80.
@num Operating cash flow 340, capex 120. Free cash flow?
= 220
! 340 − 120.
@mcq Buying a new machine for cash appears under…
+ investing activities
- operating activities
- financing activities | financing is about funding sources.
- it doesn't appear
! Long-term asset purchase.
@mcq Paying dividends appears under…
+ financing activities
- operating activities
- investing activities
- not in the cash flow statement
! Returns to owners.

## F10.9 Operating cash flow: the indirect method
Start from net profit and adjust:

```
operating cash flow = net profit
                    + depreciation (non-cash expense)
                    − increase in inventory
                    − increase in receivables
                    + increase in payables
```

Growth eats cash: more inventory and receivables tie money up.

> **Example.** Profit 100, depreciation 30, inventory +50, receivables +20,
> payables +10 → OCF = 100 + 30 − 50 − 20 + 10 = **70**.

@num Profit 80, depreciation 25, inventory up 40, receivables down 10, payables up 5. Operating cash flow?
= 80
! 80 + 25 − 40 + 10 + 5.
@num Profit 50, depreciation 10, inventory up 90. Operating cash flow?
= -30
! 50 + 10 − 90.
@mcq An increase in payables (paying suppliers later) makes operating cash flow…
+ higher
- lower
- unchanged
- negative
! Cash kept longer.

## F10.10 How the statements connect
- Net profit → retained earnings (equity) on the balance sheet.
- Depreciation → reduces PP&E (book value) and profit; added back in cash flow.
- Changes in working capital items → operating cash flow.
- Capex → PP&E up, investing cash out.
- New loans → liabilities up, financing cash in.
- Net change in cash = change in the balance-sheet cash line.

> **Example.** Retained earnings 400 at the start, net profit 90, dividends 30
> → retained earnings 460 at the end.

@num Retained earnings: start 250, net profit 70, dividends 20. End?
= 300
! 250 + 70 − 20.
@num PP&E: start 500, capex 120, depreciation 60. End?
= 560
! 500 + 120 − 60.
@num Cash at start 40, at end 65. Net cash flow of the year?
= 25
! 65 − 40.

## F10.11 Working capital in practice
**Net working capital** (operating) = inventory + receivables − payables.
It's money tied up in operations. An increase **absorbs cash**; a decrease
**releases** cash.

> **Example.** NWC goes from 150 to 210 → operations absorbed 60 of cash,
> whatever the profit.

Businesses that hold a lot of unsold stock or let customers pay late need more
financing. Selling stock turns NWC back into cash; stock held to the end of a
business's life never does.

@num Inventory 80, receivables 50, payables 40. Net working capital?
= 90
! 80 + 50 − 40.
@num NWC rises from 90 to 130. Effect on operating cash flow?
= -40
! An increase absorbs 40 of cash.
@mcq Which action releases cash from working capital?
+ selling down excess inventory
- buying more inventory
- giving customers longer to pay
- paying suppliers earlier
! Inventory turns back into cash.

## F10.12 Closing the books and the accounting cycle
The **accounting cycle**: record transactions (journal) → post to ledger →
trial balance → **adjusting entries** (accruals, depreciation, write-downs) →
financial statements → **closing entries** (revenues and expenses are zeroed
and transferred to retained earnings).

After closing, revenue and expense accounts start the new year at zero;
balance-sheet accounts carry over.

> **Example.** Year-end: revenue 900 and expenses 760 are closed → retained
> earnings +140.

@num Year-end revenues 1,200, expenses 1,050. Increase in retained earnings from closing (no dividends)?
= 150
! 1,200 − 1,050.
@mcq Which account is closed to zero at year end?
+ sales revenue
- cash | balance-sheet accounts carry over.
- equipment
- bank loan
! Income-statement accounts are temporary.
@mcq Recording this year's depreciation at year end is an example of…
+ an adjusting entry
- a closing entry
- a cash transaction
- an error
! Adjustments align the books with the accrual basis.
