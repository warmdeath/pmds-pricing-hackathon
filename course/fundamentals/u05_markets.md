# F5 · Markets and prices | Reasoning

## F5.1 What a market is
A **market** is any arrangement where buyers and sellers exchange: a street
market, an online platform, an auction, a stock exchange, a job market. What
makes it a market is that **prices** coordinate the decisions of many people
who don't know each other.

Key features to identify in any market:

- the **good** traded and how standardised it is;
- **who buys and who sells**, and how many of each;
- **how prices are set** (posted prices, negotiation, auctions);
- **what information** each side has.

> **Example.** An auction for raw materials, a shop's posted prices and a
> second-hand marketplace all match buyers and sellers, with different rules.

## F5.2 Demand: the law of demand
**Quantity demanded** = how much buyers want and can afford at a given price.
The **law of demand**: other things equal, a higher price → a lower quantity
demanded. Reasons: buyers switch to substitutes; their budget buys less; some
buyers' maximum price (willingness to pay) is exceeded.

A **demand schedule** lists price–quantity pairs; the **demand curve** plots
them (price on the vertical axis).

| price € | quantity |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 100 |
| 12 | 85 |
| 14 | 70 |

**Market demand** = sum, at each price, of all individual quantities.

## F5.3 Willingness to pay and the demand curve
Each buyer has a **willingness to pay (WTP)** — the maximum price at which she
still buys (her reservation price). If the price is at or below her WTP she
buys; otherwise she doesn't.

Sort buyers by WTP from highest to lowest: that ordered list **is** the demand
curve. At price P, quantity demanded = number of buyers with WTP ≥ P.

> **Example.** Five buyers with WTP 50, 40, 35, 20, 10. At P = 30 three buy; at
> P = 15 four buy.

The buyer's gain = WTP − price (consumer surplus, F5.11).

= 4
! Those with WTP ≥ 60: 90, 75, 60, 60.
@num WTPs: 90, 75, 60, 60, 45, 30. How many buy at a price of 50?
= 4
! 90, 75, 60, 60.
@num A buyer with WTP €120 pays €95. Her gain (consumer surplus, €)?
= 25
! 120 − 95.

## F5.4 What shifts demand
Changes in anything other than the good's own price **shift** demand:

| shifter | effect |
| --- | --- |
| income | normal goods ↑, inferior goods ↓ |
| price of a **substitute** ↑ | demand ↑ |
| price of a **complement** ↑ | demand ↓ |
| tastes, fashion, information | ↑ or ↓ |
| expectations of future prices ↑ | demand today ↑ |
| number of buyers ↑ | demand ↑ |
| season, weather | ↑ or ↓ |

> **Example.** Ice-cream demand shifts right in summer; umbrella demand shifts
> right on rainy days; demand for printer ink falls if printers become dearer.

## F5.5 Supply
**Quantity supplied** = how much sellers are willing to sell at each price.
Normally the supply curve slopes **up**: a higher price makes it worth
producing extra units whose cost is higher (the minimum price a seller
accepts for a unit is its marginal cost).

**Supply shifters**: input prices (materials, wages), technology, number of
sellers, expected prices, taxes and subsidies, disruptions.

> **Example.** If the price of chips (an input) rises, phone supply shifts left:
> at every price, fewer phones are offered.

## F5.6 Equilibrium
**Equilibrium** is the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.

- Price above equilibrium → **surplus** (unsold goods) → pressure to cut.
- Price below → **shortage** (unmet demand) → pressure to raise.

Linear case: Qd = a − bP, Qs = c + dP ⇒ P* = (a − c)/(b + d).

> **Example.** Qd = 200 − 4P, Qs = 50 + 2P ⇒ 150 = 6P ⇒ P* = 25, Q* = 100.
> At P = 30: Qd = 80, Qs = 110 → surplus 30.

= 80
! Qs = 180, Qd = 100.

## F5.8 Elasticity: how much quantity reacts
**Price elasticity of demand** = % change in quantity / % change in price.

- |ε| > 1 **elastic**: quantity reacts strongly (many substitutes).
- |ε| < 1 **inelastic**: quantity reacts weakly (necessities, few substitutes).

Revenue rule: with elastic demand, raising price **lowers** revenue; with
inelastic demand, raising price **raises** revenue.

> **Example.** Price +10 %, quantity −25 % → ε = −2.5 (elastic): revenue falls
> (1.10 × 0.75 = 0.825, −17.5 %).

## F5.9 Substitutes, complements and cross effects
**Cross-price elasticity** = % change in demand for A / % change in price of B.

- > 0: **substitutes** (Coke and Pepsi).
- < 0: **complements** (phones and cases).
- ≈ 0: unrelated.

Firms selling several products must remember cross effects: cutting the price
of one product can steal sales from another of their own products
(**cannibalisation**) or boost a complementary one.

> **Example.** Price of product B +10 %, demand for A +6 % → cross elasticity
> +0.6: substitutes.

@num Price of B rises 8 %, demand for A falls 4 %. Cross-price elasticity?
= -0.5
! −4/8.

## F5.10 Competition and market power
How much freedom a seller has over its price depends on market structure:

- **Perfect competition**: many sellers, identical products → each is a
  **price taker**.
- **Monopolistic competition**: many sellers, differentiated products →
  a little price freedom.
- **Oligopoly**: few sellers, interdependent → strategic choices.
- **Monopoly**: one seller → large price freedom, limited by demand.

**Market power** = ability to price above marginal cost without losing all
customers. It comes from differentiation, brand, location, scarcity of
competitors, switching costs.

## F5.11 Consumer and producer surplus
- **Consumer surplus** = WTP − price paid, summed over buyers.
- **Producer surplus** = price received − cost (marginal), summed over units.
- **Total surplus** = gains from trade.

With linear curves the surpluses are **triangles**: CS = ½ × (choke price −
P*) × Q*.

> **Example.** Qd = 120 − 2P (choke 60), P* = 30, Q* = 60 → CS = ½ × 30 × 60 =
> 900.

@num Three buyers with WTP 50, 40, 30 each buy one unit at price 25. Total consumer surplus?
= 45
! 25 + 15 + 5.
@num Linear demand with choke price 80; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 30. Consumer surplus?
= 450
x 900 | a triangle: multiply by ½.
! ½ × 30 × 30.
@num A seller with unit cost 12 sells 40 units at 20. Producer surplus?
= 320
! (20 − 12) × 40.

## F5.12 Specialisation and trade
**Comparative advantage**: produce what you make at the lowest **opportunity
cost**. Even someone better at everything (absolute advantage) gains by
specialising and trading.

> **Example.** In a day, Lia makes 10 tables or 20 chairs; Marco 4 tables or 16
> chairs. One table costs Lia 2 chairs, Marco 4 chairs → Lia specialises in
> tables, Marco in chairs; trading at between 2 and 4 chairs per table helps
> both.

Trade inside firms (division of labour), between firms (outsourcing) and
between countries rests on this idea.

= 2

@num Marco: 4 tables or 16 chairs. Opportunity cost of one chair in tables?
= 0.25
! 4/16.

- both
- neither
! Lower opportunity cost in chairs.
