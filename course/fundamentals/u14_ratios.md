## F14.1 Why ratios
Raw numbers don't compare: €1 m profit is great for a corner shop, terrible for
a multinational. **Ratios** scale numbers and let you compare across firms and
over time. Groups:

- **Profitability** — margins, ROA, ROE;
- **Liquidity** — current, quick ratios;
- **Efficiency** — turnover, days;
- **Leverage** — debt/equity, interest cover.

Always compare with **benchmarks**: the firm's past, competitors, industry
averages.

## F14.2 Profitability ratios
```
gross margin      = gross profit / revenue
operating margin  = EBIT / revenue
net margin        = net profit / revenue
ROA               = net profit (or EBIT) / total assets
ROE               = net profit / equity
```

> **Example.** Revenue 1,000; gross 400; EBIT 150; net 90; assets 1,200;
> equity 600 → gross 40 %, operating 15 %, net 9 %, ROA 7.5 %, ROE 15 %.

@num Net profit 60, revenue 1,200. Net margin (%)?
= 5 %
! 60/1,200.
@num Net profit 60, equity 400. ROE (%)?
= 15 %
! 60/400.
@num EBIT 180, total assets 1,500. ROA on EBIT (%)?
= 12 %
! 180/1,500.

## F14.3 Liquidity ratios
```
current ratio = current assets / current liabilities
quick ratio   = (current assets − inventory) / current liabilities
cash ratio    = cash / current liabilities
```

The quick ratio excludes inventory because stock may not be turned into cash
quickly — or at all.

> **Example.** Current assets 300 (inventory 180), current liabilities 200 →
> current 1.5, quick 0.6: comfortable only if the stock actually sells.

@num Current assets 450, current liabilities 300. Current ratio?
= 1.5
! 450/300.
@num Same, inventory 270. Quick ratio?
= 0.6
! 180/300.
@mcq A firm with current ratio 2.0 but quick ratio 0.3 depends heavily on…
+ selling its inventory
- its bank
- its receivables
- its equipment
! Most current assets are stock.

## F14.4 Efficiency: turnover and days
```
inventory turnover = COGS / average inventory
days inventory (DIO) = 365 / inventory turnover = inventory/COGS × 365
days sales outstanding (DSO) = receivables / revenue × 365
days payables outstanding (DPO) = payables / COGS × 365
asset turnover = revenue / total assets
```

> **Example.** COGS 730, inventory 146 → turnover 5, DIO 73 days.

@num COGS 1,200, average inventory 300. Inventory turnover?
= 4
! 1,200/300.
@num Same: days of inventory (365-day year)?
= 91.25
! 365/4.
@num Receivables 90, revenue 1,095. DSO (days)?
= 30
! 90/1,095 × 365.

## F14.5 The cash conversion cycle
```
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO
```

How many days a euro spent on inputs is tied up before it comes back from
customers. Shorter is better for cash; negative CCC (supermarkets, some
online retailers) means suppliers finance the business.

> **Example.** DIO 60, DSO 40, DPO 45 → CCC 55 days. If daily COGS is €2,000,
> shortening the cycle by 10 days frees about €20,000 of cash — money that can
> repay debt or fund growth without new financing.

@num DIO 45, DSO 30, DPO 50. CCC (days)?
= 25
! 45 + 30 − 50.
@num DIO 10, DSO 0, DPO 40. CCC (days)?
= -30
! Negative: suppliers finance operations.
@mcq Which change SHORTENS the cash conversion cycle?
+ selling inventory faster
- letting customers pay later
- paying suppliers earlier
- holding more stock
! Lower DIO.

## F14.6 Leverage and coverage
```
debt-to-equity      = total debt / equity
debt ratio          = total liabilities / total assets
interest coverage   = EBIT / interest expense
```

Low interest coverage (e.g. < 2) means a small fall in operating profit could
make interest unpayable.

> **Example.** EBIT 120, interest 30 → coverage 4×. Debt 400, equity 250 →
> D/E 1.6.

@num EBIT 90, interest 30. Interest coverage?
= 3
! 90/30.
@num Liabilities 600, total assets 1,000. Debt ratio (%)?
= 60 %
! 600/1,000.
@mcq Interest coverage falls from 6 to 1.2. Lenders should be…
+ more worried
- less worried
- indifferent
- pleased
! Thin cushion.

## F14.7 DuPont analysis
ROE breaks down into three drivers:

```
ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier
    = (NI/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)
```

Two firms with the same ROE can be very different: a jeweller (high margin,
low turnover) vs a discounter (low margin, high turnover) — or one that simply
borrows a lot (high multiplier).

> **Example.** Margin 5 %, turnover 2, multiplier 1.5 → ROE 15 %.

@num Net margin 4 %, asset turnover 2.5, equity multiplier 2. ROE (%)?
= 20 %
! 0.04 × 2.5 × 2.
@num ROE 18 %, net margin 6 %, multiplier 1.5. Asset turnover?
= 2
! 0.18/(0.06 × 1.5).
@mcq Raising ROE only by increasing the equity multiplier means…
+ more leverage and more risk
- better margins
- faster sales
- lower risk
! Borrowing amplifies.

## F14.8 Common-size statements
Express each line as a percentage of a base: income statement lines as % of
**revenue**, balance sheet lines as % of **total assets**. This reveals
structure and compares firms of different size.

> **Example.** Revenue 2,000, COGS 1,300, opex 500 → COGS 65 %, opex 25 %,
> operating margin 10 %. A competitor with COGS 58 % has a cost advantage of 7
> points of revenue.

@num Revenue 5,000, COGS 3,250. COGS as % of revenue?
= 65 %
! 3,250/5,000.
@num Total assets 800, inventory 200. Inventory as % of assets?
= 25 %
! 200/800.
@mcq Common-size income statements express lines as % of…
+ revenue
- total assets | that's for the balance sheet.
- equity
- net profit
! Revenue is the base.

## F14.9 Trend analysis
Look at ratios over several periods: improving or deteriorating? Use **growth
rates** and **index numbers** (base year = 100).

Warning signs: revenue grows but receivables or inventory grow much faster
(sales on easy credit, unsold stock); margins shrink while volume grows
(growth bought with discounts); cash flow below profit for several years.

> **Example.** Revenue 100 → 120 (+20 %), inventory 30 → 48 (+60 %): DIO rose;
> stock is piling up.

@num Revenue 400 → 460, receivables 50 → 70. Growth of receivables minus growth of revenue (pp)?
= 25
! 40 % − 15 %.
@num Gross margin 38 % → 33 %. Change in percentage points?
= -5
! 33 − 38.
@mcq Inventory growing much faster than sales usually signals…
+ unsold stock building up
- strong demand
- better efficiency
- lower risk
! Rising days of inventory.

## F14.10 Red flags and quality of earnings
**Quality of earnings** asks whether reported profit is backed by cash and
sustainable.

Red flags:

- operating cash flow persistently below net profit;
- rising receivables and inventory days;
- large one-off gains in "profit";
- frequent changes in accounting methods;
- capitalising costs that should be expenses;
- inventory never written down despite falling prices.

> **Example.** Net profit 50 three years running, operating cash flow 5, −10,
> 0 → profits are not turning into cash.

@num Net profits 40, 50, 60; operating cash flows 10, 15, 5. Cumulative profit minus cumulative operating cash flow?
= 120
! 150 − 30.
@mcq Which is a red flag?
+ profit rising while operating cash flow falls
- cash flow higher than profit
- stable margins
- falling debt
! Earnings not converting into cash.
@mcq A large gain from selling a building inflates this year's profit. Analysts treat it as…
+ a one-off item, not sustainable earnings
- recurring revenue
- an expense
- cash from operations
! Non-recurring.
