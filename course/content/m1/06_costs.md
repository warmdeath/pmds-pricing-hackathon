# 1.6 Production and costs

## The big picture

Behind every supply curve are **costs**. To decide how much to produce and at
what price to sell, a firm must know how its costs change with output. This
lesson builds the cost vocabulary used in every later lesson and in
managerial accounting (lesson 3.4).

> **Analogy.** A pizzeria's oven is the fixed cost — you pay for it whether
> you bake one pizza or a hundred. Flour, cheese and the pizzaiolo's overtime
> are variable costs. The question "should we bake one more pizza?" depends
> only on the cost of *that* pizza — the marginal cost.

## The production function

A **production function** Q = f(inputs) gives the maximum output from given
inputs. In the **short run** at least one input is fixed (the building, the
oven); in the **long run** all inputs can change.

- **Marginal product** (MP) of an input = extra output from one more unit of
  it. **Law of diminishing marginal returns**: adding more of one input while
  holding others fixed eventually lowers its MP (ten cooks in one small
  kitchen get in each other's way).
- **Fixed proportions (Leontief) technology**: inputs must be combined in a
  fixed recipe, Q = min(L/a, K/b). One bicycle needs exactly 2 wheels and 1
  frame; extra wheels without frames add nothing. Output is limited by the
  **scarcest input relative to its requirement** (the bottleneck).
- **Substitutable inputs** (Cobb-Douglas, Q = K^α L^β): you can trade one
  input for another.

## Cost concepts

| symbol | name | definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FC | fixed cost | does not vary with Q (rent, licences) |
| VC(Q) | variable cost | varies with Q (materials, energy) |
| TC = FC + VC | total cost | |
| AFC = FC/Q | average fixed cost | falls as Q grows |
| AVC = VC/Q | average variable cost | |
| ATC = TC/Q | average total cost | AFC + AVC |
| MC = ΔTC/ΔQ | marginal cost | cost of one more unit |

Relations worth memorising:

- MC cuts AVC and ATC at their **minimum** points: if the next unit costs less
  than the average, the average falls; if more, it rises (like a student's
  grade average and the next exam).
- AFC keeps falling ("spreading the overhead").
- **Sunk costs** (already spent, unrecoverable) are irrelevant for future
  decisions; fixed costs are not necessarily sunk (you might sublet the shop).

*Worked example.* TC = 100 + 4Q + Q². FC = 100, VC = 4Q + Q², MC = 4 + 2Q,
AVC = 4 + Q, ATC = 100/Q + 4 + Q. ATC is minimised where MC = ATC:
4 + 2Q = 100/Q + 4 + Q ⇒ Q² = 100 ⇒ Q = 10, ATC = 10 + 4 + 10 = **24** = MC.

## Constant unit costs

With a recipe and purchased inputs at fixed prices, the variable cost per unit
is simply the sum of input prices times quantities:

```
unit cost = Σ (price of input i × units of input i per output)
```

*Example.* A bicycle uses 2 wheels at €30, 1 frame at €80 and 1 chain at €10:
unit variable cost = 60 + 80 + 10 = €150. If input prices change, unit cost
changes; if the price of an input depends on how much you buy (volume
discounts, or competition for a scarce input), MC is no longer constant.

## Opportunity cost of inputs

The relevant cost of an input you already own is not what you paid for it but
what it could earn in its **best alternative use** — its opportunity cost
(lesson 1.1). Stock on hand that has no other use and no resale value has an
opportunity cost of zero; stock that could be used for a more valuable product
has the value of that alternative.

## Long run: scale

- **Economies of scale**: long-run average cost falls as output rises
  (specialisation, spreading fixed R&D, bulk buying).
- **Diseconomies of scale**: LRAC rises (coordination problems).
- **Minimum efficient scale**: the smallest output at which LRAC is minimal.
- **Economies of scope**: producing several products together is cheaper than
  separately (shared components, shared brand).

## Capacity constraints

Many firms face a hard **capacity**: no more than K units per period. Below
capacity MC may be flat; at capacity MC becomes vertical (infinite) — you
simply cannot produce more this period. Capacity turns pricing into a
rationing problem: with limited output, each unit should go where it earns the
most.

## Summary

- Short run: some inputs fixed; diminishing marginal returns raise MC.
- Know FC, VC, ATC, AVC, MC and that MC cuts the averages at their minimum.
- Fixed-proportions recipes make the scarcest input the bottleneck.
- Use **opportunity cost**, not historical cost, for inputs you already hold.
