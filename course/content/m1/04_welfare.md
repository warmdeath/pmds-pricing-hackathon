# 1.4 Welfare, taxes and price controls

## The big picture

How do we judge whether a market outcome is "good"? Welfare economics measures
the **gains from trade** with two areas on the supply-demand diagram:
consumer surplus and producer surplus. Their sum is **total surplus**; any
loss of it compared with the efficient outcome is **deadweight loss**.

> **Analogy.** Every trade is a small "win-win bonus". The buyer who would
> have paid €10 but paid €7 pockets €3 of consumer surplus; the seller whose
> cost was €4 pockets €3 of producer surplus. Welfare is the sum of all these
> bonuses — a policy that blocks trades destroys bonuses nobody gets back.

## Consumer surplus

**Consumer surplus (CS)** = willingness to pay − price actually paid, summed
over all buyers. Graphically: the area **below the demand curve and above the
price**, up to the quantity bought. For linear demand it is a triangle:

```
CS = ½ · (P_choke − P*) · Q*       where P_choke = price at which Qd = 0
```

*Worked example.* Qd = 120 − 2P (choke price 60), P\* = 30, Q\* = 60:
CS = ½ · (60 − 30) · 60 = **900**.

## Producer surplus

**Producer surplus (PS)** = price received − marginal cost, summed over units:
area **above the supply curve and below the price**. With Qs = 3P − 30
(supply starts at P = 10): PS = ½ · (30 − 10) · 60 = **600**.

Total surplus = 1,500. The competitive equilibrium **maximises total surplus**:
every unit whose value to buyers exceeds its cost is traded, and no unit whose
cost exceeds its value is (the **first welfare theorem**, under conditions —
lesson 1.10 shows where it fails).

## Taxes and incidence

A per-unit tax t drives a **wedge** between what buyers pay (P_b) and what
sellers keep (P_s = P_b − t). Quantity falls to where Qd(P_b) = Qs(P_b − t).

*Worked example.* Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 3P − 30, tax t = 10 on sellers.
120 − 2P_b = 3(P_b − 10) − 30 ⇒ 120 − 2P_b = 3P_b − 60 ⇒ P_b = 36, P_s = 26,
Q = 48. Tax revenue = 10 · 48 = 480. Buyers' price rose 6, sellers' fell 4.

**Incidence** — who really pays — does **not** depend on who legally pays the
tax, only on elasticities:

```
share borne by buyers = Es / (Es + |Ed|)    (in terms of slopes: d / (b + d))
```

Here slopes b = 2, d = 3 → buyers bear 3/5 = 60 % (6 of 10). The **less
elastic** side bears more.

**Deadweight loss (DWL)** = surplus lost because trades no longer happen:
½ · t · (Q\* − Q_tax) = ½ · 10 · (60 − 48) = **60**. DWL grows with the
**square** of the tax: doubling t roughly quadruples the DWL.

## Price controls

- **Price ceiling** (maximum price, e.g. rent control) below equilibrium →
  **shortage**: Qd > Qs. Quantity traded falls to Qs; queues, black markets,
  quality cuts and arbitrary rationing appear. Some consumers gain (those who
  get the good cheaply), producers lose, and there is a DWL.
- **Price floor** (minimum price, e.g. minimum wage, agricultural support)
  above equilibrium → **surplus**: Qs > Qd; unsold output or unemployment.
- A ceiling **above** or a floor **below** equilibrium is **non-binding**: it
  changes nothing.

*Worked example.* Ceiling P = 24 with Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 3P − 30:
Qd = 72, Qs = 42 → shortage of 30; only 42 units trade.

**Quotas** limit quantity directly; they create a gap between the demand price
and supply price at the quota quantity, and the gap is a **quota rent** for
whoever holds the licences.

## Subsidies

A per-unit subsidy is a negative tax: quantity rises above the efficient
level, buyers pay less, sellers get more, and the government's cost exceeds
the gains to buyers and sellers → also a DWL.

## Efficiency vs equity

Total surplus measures **efficiency**, not fairness. A policy can reduce
efficiency and still be chosen for **equity** (distribution) reasons. Good
analysis states both: "this policy transfers X from A to B and destroys Y".

## Summary

- CS = area under demand above price; PS = area above supply below price.
- Competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus.
- Taxes: incidence follows elasticities, not legal responsibility;
  DWL = ½·t·ΔQ.
- Binding ceilings → shortages; binding floors → surpluses.
