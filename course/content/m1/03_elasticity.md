# 1.3 Elasticity

## The big picture

Slopes depend on units (euros per kilo vs cents per gram). **Elasticity**
measures responsiveness in **percentages**, so it is unit-free and comparable
across goods: "if price rises by 1 %, by how many % does quantity change?"

> **Analogy.** Elasticity is the "stretchiness" of a rubber band. Pull on the
> price; an elastic demand stretches a lot (quantity changes a lot), an
> inelastic one barely moves.

## Price elasticity of demand

```
ε = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)
```

It is negative by the law of demand; many texts report its **absolute value**.

| |ε| | name | meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| > 1 | elastic | quantity reacts more than proportionally |
| = 1 | unit elastic | proportional reaction |
| < 1 | inelastic | quantity reacts less than proportionally |
| 0 | perfectly inelastic | vertical demand (insulin) |
| ∞ | perfectly elastic | horizontal demand (one wheat farmer) |

**Midpoint (arc) formula** — symmetric, use it between two points:

```
ε = [(Q2 − Q1) / ((Q1 + Q2)/2)] / [(P2 − P1) / ((P1 + P2)/2)]
```

*Worked example.* Price rises from €4 to €6, quantity falls from 120 to 80.
%ΔQ = −40 / 100 = −40 %; %ΔP = 2 / 5 = +40 % ⇒ ε = **−1.0** (unit elastic).
With the simple formula from the starting point you would get
(−40/120)/(2/4) = −0.67 — a different number depending on direction; that is
why the midpoint formula exists.

**Point elasticity** on a curve:  ε = (dQ/dP) · (P/Q).
For linear demand Q = a − bP:  ε = −b·P/Q. Elasticity **changes along a
straight line**: elastic at high prices (small Q), inelastic at low prices,
unit elastic exactly at the midpoint P = a/(2b).

*Worked example.* Q = 100 − 2P at P = 30: Q = 40, ε = −2·30/40 = **−1.5**.

## Elasticity and revenue

Total revenue TR = P·Q. When price rises, P goes up but Q goes down; which
wins depends on elasticity:

| demand | raise price ⇒ revenue | cut price ⇒ revenue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| elastic (|ε| > 1) | falls | rises |
| inelastic (|ε| < 1) | rises | falls |
| unit elastic | unchanged (max revenue) | unchanged |

Rule of thumb: %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP + %ΔQ. On linear demand, revenue is maximised at the
unit-elastic midpoint. Note: **revenue** maximum ≠ **profit** maximum; profit
also subtracts costs (lessons 1.6–1.8).

## What makes demand elastic?

- **Availability of substitutes** — the most important. Demand for "Brand X
  cola" is far more elastic than for "soft drinks", which is more elastic than
  for "beverages". The narrower the market definition, the more elastic.
- **Share of budget** — salt is cheap, demand inelastic; cars expensive,
  demand more elastic.
- **Necessity vs luxury.**
- **Time horizon** — demand becomes more elastic in the long run as people
  adjust (petrol: short run inelastic, long run people buy efficient cars).
- **Firm vs market** — the demand facing *one seller* of an identical product
  is much more elastic than market demand, because buyers can switch sellers.

## Other elasticities

- **Income elasticity** η = %ΔQ / %ΔIncome: η > 0 normal good (η > 1 luxury),
  η < 0 inferior good.
- **Cross-price elasticity** = %ΔQ_A / %ΔP_B: > 0 substitutes, < 0 complements,
  ≈ 0 unrelated.
- **Price elasticity of supply** = %ΔQs / %ΔP: depends on spare capacity,
  input availability and time; supply is more elastic in the long run.

*Worked example.* Income rises 10 %, demand for train tickets falls 3 %:
η = −0.3 → inferior good (people switch to cars).

## Elasticity and who bears a tax

Elasticity also decides who pays a tax (lesson 1.4): the **less elastic** side
of the market bears more of it, because it cannot escape by changing quantity.

## Summary

- Elasticity = % response of quantity to a 1 % change in a driver.
- Use the **midpoint** formula between two points, **point** elasticity on a
  curve; on linear demand elasticity changes along the line.
- Elastic: price ↑ ⇒ revenue ↓. Inelastic: price ↑ ⇒ revenue ↑.
- Substitutes and narrow market definitions make demand elastic.
