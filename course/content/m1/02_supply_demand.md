# 1.2 Demand, supply and market equilibrium

## The big picture

A **market** is any arrangement through which buyers and sellers exchange a
good. The supply-and-demand model explains how the **price** and **quantity**
traded are determined and how they react to shocks. It is the single most used
tool in economics.

> **Analogy.** Picture a crowded concert hall exit and a staircase. Demand is
> the crowd pushing to leave; supply is the width of the stairs. The price is
> like the "pressure" that builds until the flow of people matches what the
> stairs can carry.

## Demand

The **quantity demanded** is how much buyers are willing and able to buy at a
given price. The **demand curve** plots it against price.

- **Law of demand**: other things equal, when price rises the quantity
  demanded falls (curve slopes down). Reasons: the **substitution effect**
  (the good is now dearer relative to alternatives) and the **income effect**
  (your money buys less overall) — see lesson 1.5.
- Each point on the demand curve can be read the other way: the **height** of
  the curve at quantity q is the **willingness to pay** (maximum price) of the
  q-th buyer. Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands: at
  every price, add up how many units everyone wants.

**Determinants that *shift* demand** (change in demand, the whole curve moves):

| shifter | example |
| --- | --- |
| income | normal good: income ↑ → demand ↑; inferior good: income ↑ → demand ↓ |
| prices of related goods | substitutes (tea/coffee): price of coffee ↑ → demand for tea ↑; complements (printers/ink): price of printers ↑ → demand for ink ↓ |
| tastes, information, fashion | a viral video about a product |
| expectations | expected future price rise → buy now |
| number of buyers | population growth, new market opened |

A change in the good's **own price** does *not* shift the curve; it is a
**movement along** it (a change in quantity demanded).

## Supply

The **quantity supplied** is how much sellers are willing and able to sell at a
given price. The **law of supply**: higher price → more quantity supplied
(curve slopes up), because higher prices cover the higher **marginal cost** of
extra output (lesson 1.6). The height of the supply curve at q is the
**minimum price** at which the q-th unit would be offered — its marginal cost.

**Supply shifters**: input prices (cheaper chips → supply ↑), technology,
number of sellers, expectations, taxes/subsidies, weather and disruptions.

## Equilibrium

The **equilibrium** price p\* is where quantity demanded equals quantity
supplied. At any higher price there is a **surplus** (excess supply): unsold
stock pushes sellers to cut prices. At any lower price there is a
**shortage** (excess demand): buyers compete, queues form, sellers raise prices.

**Algebra.** With linear curves  Qd = a − b·P  and  Qs = c + d·P:

```
a − bP = c + dP   ⇒   P* = (a − c) / (b + d),   Q* = a − b·P*
```

*Worked example.* Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 3P − 30.
120 − 2P = 3P − 30 ⇒ 150 = 5P ⇒ **P\* = 30**, Q\* = 120 − 60 = **60**.
At P = 40: Qd = 40, Qs = 90 → surplus of 50 units.

## Comparative statics: shifting the curves

Comparative statics compares equilibria before and after a change.

| shock | price | quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| demand ↑ (shift right) | ↑ | ↑ |
| demand ↓ | ↓ | ↓ |
| supply ↑ (shift right) | ↓ | ↑ |
| supply ↓ | ↑ | ↓ |
| both ↑ | ambiguous | ↑ |
| demand ↑, supply ↓ | ↑ | ambiguous |

When both curves move, one variable's direction depends on which shift is
bigger. *Example:* a frost destroys oranges (supply ↓) while a study praises
orange juice (demand ↑): price certainly rises; quantity could go either way.

*Worked example.* Demand rises from Qd = 120 − 2P to Qd = 150 − 2P with the
same supply Qs = 3P − 30. New equilibrium: 150 − 2P = 3P − 30 ⇒ P = 36,
Q = 78. Price +6, quantity +18.

## Prices as signals

Prices carry information. A rising price tells producers "this good is
scarce relative to what people want — make more", and tells buyers
"economise". Friedrich Hayek called the price system a telecommunications
network: nobody needs to know *why* copper became scarce; the higher price is
enough to change millions of decisions.

## Markets that are not one-shot

Real markets clear repeatedly. Stocks carry over (a shop's unsold items are on
the shelf tomorrow), sellers learn from sales, and supply can react with a
**lag**. The **cobweb model** is the classic example: farmers decide this
year's planting on last year's price; a high price leads to a big harvest,
which crashes the price, which leads to a small harvest… Prices can oscillate
around the equilibrium, converging or diverging depending on the relative
slopes of supply and demand.

## Summary

- Demand slopes down, supply slopes up; their crossing gives p\* and q\*.
- Distinguish **movements along** a curve (own price changes) from **shifts**
  (other determinants).
- Surpluses push prices down, shortages push them up.
- Learn the comparative-statics table, and remember that simultaneous shifts
  leave one variable ambiguous.
