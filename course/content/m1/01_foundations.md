# 1.1 Scarcity, opportunity cost and thinking at the margin

## The big picture

Economics is the study of **how people choose when they cannot have everything**.
Resources — time, money, raw materials, attention, machine hours — are
**scarce**: there is less of them than people would like to use. Every choice
therefore means giving something up. Microeconomics studies these choices for
individual decision makers (a consumer, a firm, a market); macroeconomics
studies the economy as a whole (lessons 4.x).

Three questions organise every economy: **what** to produce, **how** to
produce it, and **for whom**. Markets answer them through prices; planned
economies answer them through orders; firms answer them internally through
budgets and managers.

> **Analogy.** Think of a phone battery at 20 %. You can watch a video, use
> navigation or keep it for a call. The battery is the scarce resource; the
> economist's question is not "what is good?" but "what is the best use of the
> *next* unit of charge?".

## Opportunity cost

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the **best alternative
you give up**. Not all the alternatives — only the best one.

*Example.* You can spend Saturday working a shift (€80) or studying. If you
study, the opportunity cost is the €80 (plus anything else the shift would
have given you). If instead the best alternative were a party you value at
€120, the opportunity cost of studying would be €120.

Key consequences:

- **Explicit costs** are money you pay (rent, materials). **Implicit costs**
  are opportunities you forgo (your own time, the interest your cash could
  earn). Economic cost = explicit + implicit.
- **Accounting profit** = revenue − explicit costs.
  **Economic profit** = revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs.
  A shop owner earning €40,000 of accounting profit who could earn €50,000 as
  an employee makes an **economic loss** of €10,000.
- **Sunk costs** are costs already paid that cannot be recovered whatever you
  do next. Because no future choice changes them, they are **not** an
  opportunity cost of any future choice. Ignoring them is rational; letting
  them drive decisions is the **sunk cost fallacy** ("we already paid for the
  concert ticket, so we must go even with a fever").

## Marginal thinking

Rational decisions are made **at the margin**: compare the **marginal benefit**
(MB) of one more unit of an activity with its **marginal cost** (MC).

- If MB > MC, do more. If MB < MC, do less. The best level is where MB = MC
  (or the last unit for which MB ≥ MC when units are whole).
- Averages mislead. A factory whose *average* cost is €10 may produce the next
  unit for €4 or for €25; only the marginal figure tells you whether that next
  unit is worth making.

*Worked example.* A bakery sells extra croissants at €1.20 each. Marginal costs
of the 1st…6th extra batch-unit are €0.50, €0.60, €0.80, €1.10, €1.40, €1.90.
It should produce the 4th unit (MB 1.20 ≥ MC 1.10) but not the 5th
(1.20 < 1.40). Total gain from the four units:
(1.20−0.50)+(1.20−0.60)+(1.20−0.80)+(1.20−1.10) = €1.80.

## The production possibilities frontier (PPF)

The **PPF** shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy (or a firm)
can produce with its resources and technology.

- Points **on** the frontier are **efficient**; points inside waste resources;
  points outside are unattainable today.
- Moving along the frontier shows the opportunity cost: producing one more
  unit of good X means producing less of good Y.
- The PPF is usually **bowed out** (concave) because resources are
  specialised: the first workers moved from farming to computers are the ones
  best at computers, so the opportunity cost of computers **rises** as you
  make more of them (**increasing opportunity cost**).
- Growth (more resources, better technology) shifts the frontier outward.

## Comparative advantage and trade

- **Absolute advantage**: producing a good with fewer resources.
- **Comparative advantage**: producing a good at a **lower opportunity cost**.

*Worked example.* In one hour Anna makes 6 pizzas or 3 cakes; Bruno makes 2
pizzas or 2 cakes. Anna has an absolute advantage in both. Opportunity costs:
Anna: 1 cake = 2 pizzas; Bruno: 1 cake = 1 pizza. Bruno gives up fewer pizzas
per cake → Bruno has the **comparative** advantage in cakes, Anna in pizzas.
If each specialises and they trade at a price between 1 and 2 pizzas per cake,
**both** gain. Trade creates value even when one side is better at everything —
this is the core insight behind specialisation inside firms and between
countries (lesson 4.6).

## Positive vs normative, models and assumptions

- **Positive** statements describe what is ("a tax on sugar raises the price
  of soda"); they can be tested.
- **Normative** statements say what should be ("sugar should be taxed"); they
  involve values.
- Economists use **models**: deliberate simplifications ("assume two goods",
  "assume firms maximise profit"). A model is like a subway map — wrong about
  distances, right about connections. **Ceteris paribus** ("other things equal")
  isolates one effect at a time.
- Standard behavioural assumption: agents are **rational** — they pursue their
  goals consistently given their information. Behavioural economics studies
  systematic departures (loss aversion, anchoring, overconfidence).

## Incentives

People respond to **incentives**: a change in costs or benefits changes
behaviour. Raising the price of a good makes buyers economise on it and sellers
supply more of it; that simple idea is the engine of the next lessons.

## Summary

- Scarcity forces choices; every choice has an **opportunity cost** — the best
  alternative forgone.
- Ignore **sunk** costs; decide by comparing **marginal** benefits and costs.
- The **PPF** shows efficiency and trade-offs; increasing opportunity cost
  bows it outward.
- **Comparative advantage** (lower opportunity cost), not absolute advantage,
  determines who should specialise in what; trade benefits both sides.
