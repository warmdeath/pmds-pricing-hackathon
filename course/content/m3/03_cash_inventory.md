# 3.3 Cash flow, working capital and inventory

## The big picture

"Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, **cash is reality**." Firms go bankrupt
when they cannot pay, not when they are unprofitable on paper. This lesson
covers the cash flow statement, working capital and inventory management —
how money gets **tied up** in stock and released by sales.

> **Analogy.** Working capital is the water in a pipe between a pump (buying
> materials) and a tap (customers paying). The longer the pipe, the more water
> sits inside instead of in your glass.

## The cash flow statement

Three sections:

1. **Operating** cash flow: net income, adjusted for non-cash items
   (+ depreciation) and changes in working capital
   (− increase in inventory, − increase in receivables, + increase in payables).
2. **Investing**: capital expenditures, acquisitions, asset sales.
3. **Financing**: borrowing, repayments, equity issued, dividends.

*Worked example (indirect method).* Net income 1,700, depreciation 300,
inventory up 2,200, payables up 0, receivables unchanged:
operating cash flow = 1,700 + 300 − 2,200 = **−200**. Growth in inventory
consumed more cash than profits generated.

**Free cash flow** = operating cash flow − capital expenditure.

## Working capital and the cash conversion cycle

```
DIO = inventory / COGS × 365          days inventory outstanding
DSO = receivables / revenue × 365     days sales outstanding
DPO = payables / COGS × 365           days payables outstanding
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO                 cash conversion cycle (days)
```

The CCC is how long a euro spent on inputs stays tied up before it returns as
cash from customers. Supermarkets often have **negative** CCC (sell before
paying suppliers); heavy-equipment makers very long ones.

*Worked example.* DIO 45, DSO 30, DPO 40 → CCC = **35 days**.

## Inventory

Inventory exists to decouple buying, producing and selling (buffer against
uncertain demand and supply). But it:

- **ties up cash** (opportunity cost of capital),
- may **become obsolete** or unsellable (its value can fall to zero),
- may require storage, insurance, handling (holding costs).

**Inventory turnover** = COGS / average inventory (times per year); higher
means stock moves faster. Days of inventory = 365 / turnover.

*Worked example.* COGS 1,200,000, average inventory 200,000 → turnover 6,
≈ 61 days of stock.

## Inventory costing: FIFO, LIFO, weighted average

When identical units were bought at different prices, which cost goes into
COGS?

- **FIFO** (first in, first out): oldest costs to COGS; ending inventory at
  recent prices.
- **LIFO** (last in, first out): newest costs to COGS (not allowed under
  IFRS).
- **Weighted average**: average cost of units available.

*Worked example.* Buy 10 units @ €20, then 10 @ €26; sell 12.
FIFO COGS = 10·20 + 2·26 = **252**; weighted average = 12·23 = **276**;
LIFO = 10·26 + 2·20 = 300. With rising prices FIFO shows higher profit.

## Inventory planning basics

- **EOQ** (economic order quantity) = √(2DS/H): D annual demand, S cost per
  order, H holding cost per unit per year — balances ordering and holding
  costs.
- **Reorder point** = demand during lead time + safety stock.
- **Just-in-time** (Toyota): minimal stock, frequent deliveries — efficient
  but fragile to disruptions.
- **Stock-outs** cost lost sales; **overstock** costs cash and risks write-offs.
  The newsvendor model (lesson 2.7) balances the two in one period.

## Liquidity and solvency

- **Current ratio** = current assets / current liabilities.
- **Quick ratio** = (current assets − inventory) / current liabilities —
  excludes stock because it may not convert to cash quickly (or at all).
- **Liquidity** = ability to pay short-term obligations; **solvency** =
  assets exceed liabilities in the long run. Insolvency leads to liquidation.

## Summary

- Profit ≠ cash; changes in working capital explain much of the gap.
- CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO measures how long cash is tied up.
- Inventory is a cash investment that may lose its value; FIFO/LIFO/average
  change reported profit, not cash.
- Liquidity ratios exclude inventory for a reason.
