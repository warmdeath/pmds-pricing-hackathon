# 3.1 The balance sheet and double-entry bookkeeping

## The big picture

Accounting is the **language of business**: a standard way to record what a
firm owns, owes and earns. Three statements tell the story — the **balance
sheet** (a photo at one moment), the **income statement** (a film of
performance over a period, lesson 3.2) and the **cash flow statement** (where
the cash came from and went, lesson 3.3).

> **Analogy.** The balance sheet is a photo of your wardrobe on 31 December:
> what's in it and who lent you each item. The income statement is the diary
> of the year: what you earned and spent.

## The accounting equation

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

- **Assets**: resources the firm controls that will bring future benefits —
  cash, receivables (customers owe you), **inventory** (raw materials, work in
  progress, finished goods), equipment, buildings, patents.
- **Liabilities**: obligations to outsiders — payables (you owe suppliers),
  loans, bonds, taxes due.
- **Equity**: the owners' residual claim = assets − liabilities. Grows with
  owners' contributions and retained profits.

The equation always balances because every asset was financed by someone:
creditors (liabilities) or owners (equity).

## Classification

| assets | liabilities & equity |
| --- | --- |
| **current** (≤ 1 year): cash, receivables, inventory | **current liabilities**: payables, short-term debt |
| **non-current**: property, plant & equipment (net of depreciation), intangibles | **non-current liabilities**: long-term loans, bonds |
| | **equity**: share capital, retained earnings |

**Working capital** = current assets − current liabilities (lesson 3.3).

## Double-entry bookkeeping

Every transaction affects **at least two accounts** so the equation stays
balanced. Conventionally each entry has a **debit** (left) and a **credit**
(right) of equal amounts:

- Assets and expenses increase with **debits**.
- Liabilities, equity and revenues increase with **credits**.

*Worked examples* (start: owners put in €12,000 cash):

| transaction | effect |
| --- | --- |
| owners contribute 12,000 cash | cash +12,000 / share capital +12,000 |
| buy materials 2,000 in cash | inventory +2,000 / cash −2,000 (assets swap; total unchanged) |
| buy materials 1,000 on credit | inventory +1,000 / payables +1,000 |
| sell goods costing 800 for 2,500 cash | cash +2,500, revenue +2,500 ; COGS +800, inventory −800 → equity +1,700 via profit |
| pay supplier 1,000 | payables −1,000 / cash −1,000 |

After these: cash 12,000 − 2,000 + 2,500 − 1,000 = 11,500; inventory
2,000 + 1,000 − 800 = 2,200; payables 0; equity 12,000 + 1,700 = 13,700.
Check: assets 11,500 + 2,200 = 13,700 = liabilities 0 + equity 13,700 ✔.

## Valuation: historical cost, fair value, prudence

Assets are usually recorded at **historical cost** (what was paid), sometimes
at fair value. The **prudence** principle and the rule **lower of cost and net
realisable value** require writing inventory down if it can no longer be sold
for at least its cost. Inventory that can never be sold is worth **zero** —
whatever it cost.

## Depreciation

Long-lived assets are expensed gradually: **straight-line depreciation** =
(cost − residual value)/useful life per year. A €10,000 machine with a 5-year
life and no residual value costs €2,000 per year; its **book value** falls
10,000 → 8,000 → … → 0.

## Accounting principles

Accrual basis (record revenues when earned, expenses when incurred — lesson
3.2), going concern, consistency, matching, materiality. In the EU, listed
groups use **IFRS**; Italian companies follow the Codice Civile and OIC
standards (Bocconi courses call the income statement *Conto Economico* and the
balance sheet *Stato Patrimoniale*).

## Summary

- Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always.
- Every transaction has two sides (debit = credit).
- Current vs non-current; working capital = CA − CL.
- Inventory at lower of cost and net realisable value; unsellable stock is
  worth nothing.
