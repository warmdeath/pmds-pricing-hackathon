# 3.4 Managerial accounting: break-even, contribution margin, relevant costs

## The big picture

Financial accounting reports to outsiders; **managerial accounting** supports
internal decisions: what to produce, at what price, whether to accept an
order, which product to prioritise when a resource is scarce. Its golden rule:
**use relevant costs** — future costs that differ between the alternatives.

> **Analogy.** When choosing between two trains, the ticket you already bought
> for a missed train is irrelevant; only the prices and times of the trains
> still available matter.

## Cost behaviour

- **Variable costs** change in proportion to volume (materials).
- **Fixed costs** don't change within a relevant range (rent, salaries).
- **Mixed costs** have both parts.
- **Direct** costs are traceable to a product; **indirect** (overhead) must
  be allocated — and allocations can mislead decisions.

## Contribution margin and break-even

```
unit contribution margin   CM = P − VC
contribution margin ratio  CM / P
break-even quantity        Q_BE = FC / CM
break-even revenue         FC / (CM ratio)
target profit quantity     (FC + target) / CM
margin of safety           (actual sales − break-even sales) / actual sales
```

*Worked example.* Price €25, variable cost €10, fixed costs €30,000:
CM = 15, Q_BE = **2,000 units**, break-even revenue €50,000. To earn €15,000:
(30,000 + 15,000)/15 = 3,000 units.

**Operating leverage** = contribution margin / operating profit: high fixed
costs amplify the effect of volume changes on profit.

## Multi-product break-even and sales mix

With several products, use the **weighted average CM** given the sales mix:
products A (CM 10) and B (CM 30) sold 3:1 → weighted CM = (3·10 + 1·30)/4 =
15 per unit.

## Relevant costs and special decisions

Relevant = **future** and **differential**. Irrelevant: sunk costs, costs
identical across options, allocated fixed overheads that won't change.

- **Special order**: accept if price > relevant (incremental) cost, given
  spare capacity. With no spare capacity, add the opportunity cost of the
  displaced sales.
- **Make or buy**, **keep or drop** a product line: compare avoidable costs
  vs lost contribution.
- **Opportunity cost of materials**: materials already in stock are valued at
  their best alternative use or resale value, not their purchase price.

*Worked example.* A firm holds 100 kg of a material bought at €8/kg; it has
no other use and no resale value. For a special order needing it, the
relevant material cost is **€0**. If instead it could be sold for €5/kg, the
relevant cost is €5/kg.

## Scarce resources: contribution per unit of the constraint

When a resource limits output (machine hours, a scarce component, shelf
space), rank products by **contribution margin per unit of the scarce
resource**, not by CM per unit of product.

*Worked example.* 100 machine hours available.
Product X: CM €40, uses 4 h → €10/h. Product Y: CM €24, uses 2 h → €12/h.
Y earns more per scarce hour: 50 units of Y → €1,200, versus 25 units of
X → €1,000 — although X has the higher CM per unit. If demand for Y is
capped (say 30 units), make 30 Y (60 h) and use the remaining 40 h for
10 X: 720 + 400 = €1,120.

With several constraints at once, the problem becomes **linear programming**
(maximise Σ CM_i·x_i subject to resource constraints); the bottleneck can
shift as the mix changes (Theory of Constraints, Goldratt).

## Pricing approaches

- **Cost-plus pricing**: price = cost × (1 + markup). Simple, ignores demand.
- **Value-based pricing**: price from customers' willingness to pay.
- **Competition-based pricing**: price relative to rivals.
- **Target costing**: start from the market price, subtract the required
  margin, design the product to that cost.
Economics (lesson 1.8) says the profit-maximising price depends on **both**
marginal cost **and** demand elasticity; cost-plus with a fixed markup is
optimal only if elasticity happens to match the markup.

## Summary

- CM = P − VC; break-even Q = FC/CM.
- Decide with relevant (future, differential) costs; ignore sunk costs; value
  stocks at opportunity cost.
- With a scarce resource, rank by CM per unit of that resource.
- Cost-plus, value-based, competition-based and target costing.
