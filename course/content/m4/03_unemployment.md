# 4.3 Unemployment and the business cycle

## The big picture

Economies do not grow smoothly: they alternate **expansions** and
**recessions** — the **business cycle**. Recessions show up most painfully as
**unemployment**. This lesson defines the labour-market statistics, the types
of unemployment, and the regularities of cycles.

> **Analogy.** The economy is like a tide on top of a slowly rising sea level:
> the sea level is long-run growth (potential output), the tide is the
> business cycle.

## Measuring unemployment

- **Labour force** = employed + unemployed (people without a job who are
  actively looking and available).
- **Unemployment rate** = unemployed / labour force.
- **Participation rate** = labour force / working-age population.
- **Employment rate** = employed / working-age population.
- People who stop searching (**discouraged workers**) leave the labour force,
  so unemployment can fall for bad reasons.

*Worked example.* Working-age population 50 m, employed 23 m, unemployed 2 m:
labour force 25 m, unemployment rate **8 %**, participation **50 %**,
employment rate 46 %.

## Types of unemployment

- **Frictional**: time spent between jobs, searching and matching.
- **Structural**: mismatch of skills, location or wages (declining
  industries, minimum wages above productivity, rigid labour laws).
- **Cyclical**: caused by recessions (deficient demand).

The **natural rate** (≈ NAIRU) = frictional + structural; it is the rate
consistent with stable inflation. Unemployment above it = slack.

## The business cycle

- Phases: expansion → peak → recession (contraction) → trough → recovery.
- A rough rule calls two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP a
  "technical recession".
- **Output gap** = (actual − potential GDP)/potential.
- **Okun's law**: each 1 % of negative output gap raises unemployment above
  its natural rate by roughly ½ point (coefficient varies by country).
- **Phillips curve**: in the short run, lower unemployment goes with higher
  inflation; expectations-augmented version:
  π = π_expected − β(u − u_natural). The trade-off vanishes in the long run.

*Worked example (Okun).* Natural rate 7 %, output gap −4 %, coefficient 0.5 →
u ≈ 7 + 0.5·4 = **9 %**.

## Leading, coincident and lagging indicators

- Leading: purchasing managers' indexes (PMI), new orders, building permits,
  stock prices, the yield curve, consumer confidence.
- Coincident: industrial production, retail sales, employment.
- Lagging: unemployment duration, inflation.

## Seasonality vs cycles

Many series have predictable **seasonal** patterns (retail sales in December,
tourism in August) that statistical offices remove ("seasonally adjusted").
Cycles are irregular in length and depth; seasonality repeats with a fixed
period. Distinguishing the two matters for any forecast: a December sales
spike says nothing about the trend.

## Summary

- Unemployment rate = unemployed/labour force; watch participation too.
- Frictional, structural, cyclical; natural rate = frictional + structural.
- Okun links the output gap to unemployment; Phillips links unemployment to
  inflation (short run only).
- Separate seasonal patterns from cyclical movements.
