# 4.5 Central banks and monetary policy

## The big picture

Central banks — the **ECB** for the euro area, the Fed in the US, the Banca
d'Italia as part of the Eurosystem — control the price of short-term money.
Through it they influence borrowing, spending, asset prices, exchange rates
and, ultimately, inflation. This lesson explains the tools and the
transmission mechanism.

> **Analogy.** The policy rate is a thermostat: raise it to cool an
> overheating economy, lower it to warm a cold one. The house reacts slowly,
> so the thermostat must be set by forecasting, not by the current
> temperature.

## Mandate and independence

- The ECB's primary mandate: **price stability**, defined as 2 % inflation
  over the medium term (symmetric). The Fed has a **dual mandate**
  (prices and maximum employment).
- **Independence** from governments improves credibility: it prevents
  printing money to finance deficits and anchors inflation expectations.

## Tools

- **Policy interest rates**: the ECB's deposit facility rate, main
  refinancing rate, marginal lending rate. They set the floor and ceiling for
  overnight interbank rates (€STR).
- **Open market operations**: lending to banks against collateral.
- **Reserve requirements**.
- **Unconventional tools**: **quantitative easing** (large-scale purchases of
  bonds to lower long-term yields: APP, PEPP), **forward guidance**
  (communicating future policy), targeted long-term loans (TLTROs), negative
  rates (ECB 2014–2022), the TPI to limit unwarranted spreads.

## Transmission mechanism

Policy rate ↑ →

1. **Interest-rate channel**: loans and mortgages dearer → investment and
   durable consumption ↓.
2. **Credit channel**: banks lend less, collateral values fall.
3. **Asset-price/wealth channel**: bond and stock prices ↓ → wealth ↓.
4. **Exchange-rate channel**: currency appreciates → exports ↓, import
   prices ↓.
5. **Expectations channel**: credible tightening lowers expected inflation.

Lags are long and variable (often 12–24 months to the peak effect on
inflation).

## The Taylor rule

A description of how central banks often behave:

```
i = r* + π + 0.5·(π − π*) + 0.5·(output gap)
```

*Worked example.* r\* = 1 %, π = 4 %, π\* = 2 %, output gap 0:
i = 1 + 4 + 1 + 0 = **6 %**.

## Limits

- **Zero (effective) lower bound**: rates can't go much below zero → QE and
  forward guidance.
- **Liquidity trap**, **time inconsistency** (temptation to inflate later),
  supply shocks that force a choice between output and inflation.
- In a **monetary union** one policy rate must fit countries with different
  cycles — a key challenge for the euro area.

## Recent history in one paragraph

2008 financial crisis → rate cuts and QE; 2010–2012 euro debt crisis →
"whatever it takes" (Draghi) and OMT; 2014–2021 low inflation → negative rates
and QE; 2022–2023 energy shock and post-pandemic demand → fastest hiking cycle
in ECB history (deposit rate from −0.5 % to 4 %); 2024–2025 easing as
inflation fell back towards 2 %.

## Summary

- Central banks steer short-term rates; ECB targets 2 % inflation.
- Tools: policy rates, open market operations, QE, forward guidance.
- Transmission via rates, credit, assets, exchange rates, expectations —
  with long lags.
- Taylor rule: i = r\* + π + 0.5(π − π\*) + 0.5·gap.
