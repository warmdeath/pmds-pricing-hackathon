# 4.6 The open economy: trade and exchange rates

## The big picture

Countries trade goods, services and capital. Trade lets countries specialise
by **comparative advantage** (lesson 1.1); capital flows let savings go where
returns are highest. Exchange rates connect price levels and interest rates
across currencies.

> **Analogy.** International trade is the same as two flatmates splitting
> chores: each does what they are relatively better at, and both end up with
> more free time — even if one is faster at everything.

## Why trade

- **Comparative advantage** (Ricardo): specialise in goods with the lowest
  opportunity cost; trade at intermediate prices; both gain.
- **Heckscher-Ohlin**: countries export goods that use their abundant factors
  (capital-rich countries export capital-intensive goods).
- **New trade theory** (Krugman): economies of scale and love of variety
  explain trade between similar countries in similar goods (Italian and
  German cars).
- Gains are aggregate; some groups lose (import-competing workers) → the
  politics of trade.

*Worked example.* Italy: 1 unit of wine costs 2 units of machines; Germany:
1 wine costs 4 machines. Italy has the comparative advantage in wine; any
price between 2 and 4 machines per wine benefits both.

## Trade policy

- **Tariffs** (taxes on imports) raise domestic prices, protect domestic
  producers, raise revenue, and create DWL (lesson 1.4) — plus retaliation.
- **Quotas**, subsidies, standards and non-tariff barriers.
- The EU is a customs union with a single market and common trade policy;
  the WTO sets global rules.

## Balance of payments

- **Current account** = trade balance (goods and services) + primary income +
  transfers.
- **Financial account**: net purchases of foreign assets.
- They mirror each other: a current-account surplus (e.g. Germany, and Italy
  in recent years) means the country is lending to the rest of the world.
- **S − I = NX**: national saving minus investment equals net exports.

## Exchange rates

- **Nominal exchange rate**: price of one currency in another (EUR/USD 1.10 =
  1 euro buys 1.10 dollars).
- **Appreciation** of the euro → European goods dearer abroad, imports
  cheaper.
- **Real exchange rate** = e · P_domestic / P_foreign — competitiveness.
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)**: in the long run identical baskets should
  cost the same across countries (the Big Mac index).
- **Interest rate parity**: expected depreciation ≈ interest differential.
- Regimes: floating, fixed/pegged, currency union (the euro: no devaluation
  possible between members → adjustment through prices, wages and fiscal
  transfers).

*Worked example.* A €40 Italian bag sells in the US at the dollar price
40 × 1.10 = **$44**. If the euro appreciates to 1.25: **$50** — 13.6 % dearer
for American buyers with no change in the Italian price.

## Global value chains

Modern products are made across many countries (design here, chips there,
assembly elsewhere). Disruptions in one input (semiconductors in 2021) can
halt production of finished goods worldwide: output is limited by the
scarcest component, exactly like a fixed-proportions recipe (lesson 1.6).

## Summary

- Trade follows comparative advantage, factor abundance and scale.
- Tariffs protect some groups at a net cost.
- Current account = S − I; mirrored by financial flows.
- Exchange rates: nominal vs real; PPP and interest parity in the long run.
