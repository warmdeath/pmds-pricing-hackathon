# 4.4 Aggregate demand and supply, fiscal policy

## The big picture

The **AD-AS model** is the macro counterpart of supply and demand: aggregate
demand (total spending) and aggregate supply (total production) determine the
price level and real output. It explains booms, recessions, inflation shocks
and what governments and central banks can do about them.

> **Analogy.** AD is the economy's appetite, AS its kitchen. A bigger appetite
> with a kitchen already at full capacity just means higher prices on the
> menu.

## Aggregate demand

AD = C + I + G + NX at each price level; it slopes down (higher prices reduce
real wealth, raise interest rates, make exports less competitive).

Shifts: consumer and business confidence, fiscal policy (G, taxes), monetary
policy (interest rates), world demand, exchange rates, wealth (house and stock
prices).

## Aggregate supply

- **Short-run AS** slopes up: some prices and wages are sticky, so higher
  demand raises output and prices.
- **Long-run AS** is vertical at **potential output** Y\*: determined by
  labour, capital, technology and institutions, not by prices.
- Supply shocks (energy prices, pandemics, productivity) shift SRAS.

## Short-run vs long-run outcomes

| shock | short run | long run (no policy) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AD ↓ (confidence falls) | output ↓, prices ↓ (recession) | wages/prices adjust, back to Y\* at lower prices |
| AD ↑ | output ↑, prices ↑ | back to Y\*, higher prices |
| SRAS ↓ (oil shock) | output ↓, prices ↑ — **stagflation** | slow recovery |
| LRAS ↑ (productivity) | output ↑, prices ↓ | growth |

## Fiscal policy and the multiplier

Government spending and taxes shift AD. Initial spending becomes someone's
income, part of which is spent again — the **multiplier**:

```
spending multiplier  = 1 / (1 − MPC)
tax multiplier       = −MPC / (1 − MPC)
balanced budget multiplier = 1
```

MPC = marginal propensity to consume. With MPC = 0.8: spending multiplier
**5**, tax multiplier −4. In an open economy with taxes the multiplier is much
smaller: 1/(1 − MPC(1 − t) + m), m = marginal propensity to import.

*Worked example.* MPC 0.75, tax rate 0.2, import propensity 0.1:
1/(1 − 0.75·0.8 + 0.1) = 1/0.5 = **2**. €10 bn of extra spending → ≈ €20 bn
of GDP in this simple model.

Real-world estimates are usually 0.5–1.5 and depend on the cycle (larger in
deep recessions, especially at zero interest rates), on **crowding out**
(higher rates displace private investment) and on **Ricardian equivalence**
(households save tax cuts expecting future taxes).

## Deficits and debt

- **Deficit** = spending − revenue in a year (a flow); **debt** = accumulated
  deficits (a stock).
- Debt dynamics: the debt/GDP ratio falls when the primary surplus is large
  enough or when growth g exceeds the interest rate r:
  Δd ≈ (r − g)·d − primary surplus.
- EU fiscal rules (Stability and Growth Pact): deficit ≤ 3 % and debt ≤ 60 %
  of GDP as reference values; Italy's debt is about 135–140 % of GDP.
- **Automatic stabilisers** (unemployment benefits, progressive taxes)
  dampen cycles without new decisions.

## Summary

- AD slopes down, SRAS up, LRAS vertical at potential output.
- Demand shocks move output and prices together; supply shocks move them in
  opposite directions (stagflation).
- Multiplier 1/(1 − MPC) in the simplest model; smaller in reality.
- Debt dynamics depend on r − g and the primary balance.
